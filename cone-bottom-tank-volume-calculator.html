Cone Bottom Tank FAQ

How do I size a conical fermenter for a 7-gallon batch?▼

Work backwards from headspace. A 7-gallon batch is 1,617 cubic inches, and active fermentation needs 20–30% free room for krausen, so target a brimful shell near 9.5 gallons. A typical stainless conical in this class measures about 13.7 in inside diameter with a 13.5 in straight wall and a 60° cone dropping to a 2-in dump valve: cylinder 1,990 in³ + cone 194 in³ = 2,184 in³ = 9.45 gal. Your 7-gal batch then sits at 74% fill, and the cone itself holds about 0.84 gal of trub and yeast below the racking port.

What is the Excel formula for conical bottom tank volume?▼

Put the top radius in B1 (12), cone height in B2 (6.35), outlet radius in B3 (1) and cylinder height in B4 (36). Cone gallons: =PI()*B2/3*(B1^2+B1*B3+B3^2)/231, which returns 4.52 gal for those numbers. Cylinder gallons: =PI()*B1^2*B4/231, which returns 70.5 gal. Add the two cells for total capacity, and multiply by 3.78541 for liters. The full spreadsheet layout with the partial-level formula is in the Excel section of this page.

Should I choose a 45° or 60° cone angle?▼

On a 24-in diameter tank draining through a 2-in outlet, a 45° cone needs a cone height of (12 − 1)/1 = 11.0 in and holds 7.83 gal; a 60° cone needs only (12 − 1)/1.7321 = 6.35 in and holds 4.52 gal. The 60° tank is about 4.7 in shorter but self-cleans, because most slurries and yeast slide down a 60° wall without help. A 30° cone needs 19.05 in of height and holds 13.56 gal — the tank grows roughly 13 in taller for the same cylinder, so it is rare below industrial silo sizes.

How do I find the liquid volume at any level in a cone bottom tank?▼

Measure depth from the cone tip, not from the floor. If the level is inside the cone (level ≤ cone height), compute the liquid radius r_lvl = r + (R − r) × (level ÷ h), then use the frustum formula with r_lvl as the top radius. If the level is above the cone, add the full cone volume to the cylinder volume πR² × (level − h). On the 24-in reference tank, 20 in of depth gives 1,044 + 6,175 = 7,219 in³ = 31.25 gal, which is 41.7% of the 75-gal total.

How is a cone bottom tank different from a flat bottom tank for volume?▼

Two differences: the math and the usable water. A flat-bottom vertical tank is a single formula, πR²h, while a cone-bottom tank splits into a cylinder plus a truncated cone with two radii. And the cone section is rarely full of clean water — it is the settling zone where sediment, yeast or sludge collects. On the standard poly tanks charted on this page, the cone represents roughly 11–15% of nameplate capacity: 7.8 gal of a 55-gal tank, 15.3 gal of a 100-gal tank. You trade that geometry for the ability to drain solids out of a 2-in outlet instead of digging them out of a flat floor.

What is the partial volume formula inside the cone section?▼

A level inside the cone is a smaller frustum: the bottom radius stays the outlet radius r, and the top radius grows linearly with depth, r_lvl = r + (R − r) × level/h. Then V = (π × level ÷ 3) × (r_lvl² + r_lvl × r + r²). Example on the 24-in tank with a 2-in outlet: at 3 in of depth, r_lvl = 1 + 11 × (3 ÷ 6.35) = 6.20 in, so V = π × 3 ÷ 3 × (38.4 + 6.2 + 1) = 143 in³ = 0.62 gal. A flat-bottom cylinder of the same diameter would hold 5.9 gal at that depth — nearly ten times more — which is why you never scale cone volume linearly.

How do I calculate a rectangular tank with a sloped bottom?▼

Use the prism formula with average depth: V = L × W × (h1 + h2) ÷ 2, where h1 and h2 are the shallow and deep end depths. A 48 × 24 in footprint sloping from 12 in to 18 in holds 48 × 24 × 15 = 17,280 in³ = 74.8 gal (283 liters). The formula assumes the slope runs across the full length. If only half the floor slopes, split the tank into a flat box plus a wedge and add the two volumes.

Is a hopper bottom the same as a cone bottom?▼

Both are truncated shapes, but a hopper is pyramidal — rectangular at top and bottom — while a cone is circular. The pyramidal frustum formula is V = h ÷ 6 × (A1 + A2 + 4 × Am), where A1 and A2 are the top and bottom areas and Am is the mid-height area. A square hopper tapering from 24 × 24 in to 4 × 4 in over 12 in of depth holds 12 ÷ 6 × (576 + 16 + 4 × 196) = 2,752 in³ = 11.9 gal. Hoppers suit rectangular process floors and dry solids; round cones suit liquids and yeast because the walls have no corners to pack.

What standard sizes do cone bottom tanks come in?▼

Rotationally molded poly cone-bottom tanks cluster at 15, 30, 55 and 100 gallons, almost always with 45° cones and 2-in outlets — the chart on this page lists realistic dimensions for each. Stainless conical fermenters run 7, 14 and 28 gallons for homebrew and pilot scale. Craft breweries step up in barrels: 1 bbl = 31 gal, so a 1-bbl unitank around 20 in in diameter with a 60° cone holds about 31 gallons brimful, and a 7-bbl system is roughly 42 in across with a cone section near 7.5 gal.

How accurate is the frustum formula on a real tank?▼

Expect the geometry to be within ±2–3% of the nameplate on a molded or rolled tank: a nominal 55-gal cone-bottom tank typically measures 54–56 gal brimful by calculation, and roto-molding wall variation accounts for most of the gap. Three corrections push the real number lower: the outlet nozzle and flush valve add displaced volume below the cone, interior fittings like spray balls and thermowells take another fraction of a gallon, and any level gauge should be calibrated against calculated dip values rather than the sticker. For dosing chemicals or billing water, verify one point with a meter and adjust the chart.

Cone Tank Volume Calculation: The Frustum Formula in Plain Numbers

A cone bottom tank is never one shape — it is a vertical cylinder sitting on a truncated cone. That is why a cone tank volume calculation needs two formulas and four measurements: the cylinder inside diameter D, the cylinder height H, the vertical cone height h, and the outlet diameter at the drain (whose radius we call r). The top radius of the cone is simply R = D ÷ 2. The truncated cone — a frustum — is the piece people get wrong, because the pointed-cone formula they remember from school does not fit a tank that stops at a 2-in drain.

The frustum formula in plain numbers: multiply π × h ÷ 3 by the sum of R², R×r and r². In symbols, V = πh/3 (R² + Rr + r²). Everything is in inches and cubic inches; divide by 231 to get US gallons, because 1 gallon = 231 cubic inches exactly. The cylinder above it is the familiar πR²H.

The three-term sum looks strange until you see what it averages. A frustum is a cone with its tip sliced off, so its cross-section sweeps from the big area πR² at the top to the small area πr² at the outlet. The middle term R×r is the geometric mean of the two end areas, and averaging the three gives the exact mean cross-section for a linear taper — no calculus required, and no approximation error either. That is also why the outlet diameter belongs in the inputs: forgetting the 2-in outlet and treating r as zero silently converts your frustum into a pointed cone and shifts the cone section by up to 8%.

Worked example: 24-in tank, 2-in outlet, 60° cone

Inside diameter D = 24 in → R = 12 in

Outlet diameter 2 in → r = 1 in

60° cone height: h = (12 − 1) ÷ 1.7321 = 6.35 in

R² + R·r + r² = 144 + 12 + 1 = 157

Cylinder height H = 36 in

Cone: π × 6.35 ÷ 3 × 157 = π × 6.650 × 157 = 1,044 in³

1,044 ÷ 231 = 4.52 gal in the cone

Cylinder: π × 144 × 36 = 16,286 in³ = 70.5 gal

Total = 17,330 in³ = 75.0 gal = 284 L

The cone is only 6% of the numbers here — but it is the part that drains, settles and cleans.

⚠️ Warning: matching the formula to the real shell matters more than the arithmetic. The pointed-cone shortcut V = πR²h/3 assumes the shell narrows to zero radius, but a drainable tank stops at the outlet — on this 24-in tank it returns 957 in³ = 4.15 gal against the true 1,044 in³ = 4.52 gal. The cruder habit of squaring the whole diameter, D²h/3 ÷ 3, returns 1,219 in³ = 5.28 gal — a 17% overestimate of the cone section. Half a gallon of cone error is invisible on a chart and very visible when you order 5,000 gallons of treatment chemical by it.

💡 Tip: measure the cone height vertically, from the plane of the cylinder wall straight down to the outlet face — never along the slanted wall. On a 60° cone the slant is exactly twice the vertical height: 12.70 in of wall vs 6.35 in of rise. Feed the slant into the frustum formula and the cone section doubles to 9.0 gal, because volume scales linearly with h.

Calculate Volume in a Sloped Bottom Tank: The Prism Formula

Rectangular tanks and basins with one sloping side — hopper-style floors under a fixed roof, tanks set on graded pads, fermentation trays — use a much friendlier formula than the frustum. When the floor slopes linearly from depth h1 at one end to depth h2 at the other, the cross-section is a trapezoid and the tank is a prism: V = L × W × (h1 + h2) ÷ 2. In words: footprint times average depth.

Worked example: sloped-bottom tank, 48 × 24 in footprint

Length L = 48 in, width W = 24 in

Shallow end h1 = 12 in, deep end h2 = 18 in

Average depth = (12 + 18) ÷ 2 = 15 in

V = 48 × 24 × 15 = 17,280 in³

÷ 231 = 74.8 US gal

× 3.78541 ÷ gal → 283 liters

Two cautions keep the prism formula honest. First, it assumes the slope runs across the entire length; if only half the floor slopes, split the tank into a flat box plus a wedge and add the two volumes — the wedge alone is L × W × (h2 − h1) ÷ 2. Second, average depth only works for a single straight slope; a V-shaped double slope has two wedges and no flat box. For tanks that mix cylindrical and rectangular sections, the water tank capacity calculator covers the common storage geometries side by side.

Here is the split method with numbers. Take the same 48 × 24 in footprint, but suppose only the last 24 in of length slopes, rising from 12 in to 18 in. The flat section is 24 × 24 × 12 = 6,912 in³ = 29.9 gal. The sloped section is a wedge: 24 × 24 × (18 − 12) ÷ 2 = 1,728 in³ = 7.5 gal. Total 8,640 in³ = 37.4 gal — barely half of the 74.8 gal full-slope answer, which shows how quickly the wrong assumption about where the slope lives distorts a sloped-bottom estimate. When the slope runs across the width instead of the length, the formula is identical with L and W swapped, because the prism formula only cares about footprint area and the two end depths.

Frustum Gallon Tank Calculator: Gallon Conversions and Standard Sizes

Any frustum gallon tank calculator lives or dies by three conversion constants: 1 US gallon = 231 cubic inches exactly, 1 gallon = 3.78541 liters, and 1 cubic inch = 0.004329 gallons. Convert after you compute volume in cubic inches — not before — because rounding early on a 100-gal tank drifts the answer by most of a gallon. The cone section of a standard poly tank is a bigger share of capacity than most buyers expect: roughly 11–15% of nameplate, which is exactly the volume you are trading away in exchange for drainability.

Notice the pattern in the table: as diameter grows, the 45° cone deepens proportionally, so the cone share of capacity stays near 15%. Switch the same shells to 60° cones and the cone share drops to 9–11% while total height shrinks by 3–5 in per size — the trade-off the angle FAQ below quantifies.

One conversion trap deserves its own paragraph: imperial gallons. A UK imperial gallon is 277.42 cubic inches against the US 231 — about 20% larger — so a 55-gal cone-bottom tank quoted by a British supplier is not the same vessel as an American one. The 75-gal reference tank on this page holds 284 liters; expressed in imperial gallons that is only 62.5. Chemical dosing rates printed in liters per hectare or per 1,000 liters sidestep the whole mess, which is why the calculator shows liters next to every gallon figure — 15 gal = 56.8 L, 55 gal = 208.2 L, 100 gal = 378.5 L — and why you should confirm which gallon a spec sheet means before ordering liners, heaters or treatment.

Conical Fermenter and Brewing Case Studies

Brewing is where cone-bottom geometry earns its keep, and where the frustum math gets checked against real hardware every batch. Case one: the classic 7-gallon homebrew conical. Shells in this class measure about 13.7 in inside diameter with a 13.5 in straight wall, plus a 60° cone dropping to a 2-in dump valve. Run the numbers: cylinder π × 6.85² × 13.5 = 1,990 in³; cone height (6.85 − 1) ÷ 1.7321 = 3.38 in, so cone volume = π × 3.38 ÷ 3 × (46.9 + 6.85 + 1) = 194 in³. Brimful total: 2,184 in³ = 9.45 gal. A 7-gal batch fills it to 74%, leaving 2.45 gal of krausen headspace — which is exactly why a “7-gallon” fermenter is never 7 gallons brimful.

The cone itself holds 0.84 gal. That is the working zone: during fermentation, trub and dead yeast compact against the 60° wall and slide to the dump valve, so the first gallon you draw from the bottom is solids, not beer. Pull it, and the beer above the cone stays on clean yeast. A flat-bottom fermenter of the same capacity traps that sediment across the whole floor, where racking picks it back up.

Conical fermenters — the cone is the yeast harvest zonePhoto: Unsplash

Case two scales the same logic to commercial units, where volumes are quoted in barrels: 1 bbl = 31 US gal = 117 liters. A 1-bbl unitank built around a 20-in inside diameter with a 20.9 in straight wall and a 60° cone to a 1.5-in tri-clamp outlet (r = 0.75 in) computes as follows: cone height = (10 − 0.75) ÷ 1.7321 = 5.34 in; cone volume = π × 5.34 ÷ 3 × (100 + 7.5 + 0.56) = 604 in³ = 2.62 gal; cylinder 6,566 in³ = 28.4 gal. Total 31.05 gal — a 1-bbl tank within rounding of its nameplate, with a wetted height near 26 in. A 7-bbl system follows the same recipe at roughly 42 in diameter, and its cone section holds about 7.5 gal of yeast worth harvesting every generation.

Hopper and Settling Zone: Why the Cone Is Working Volume

In a water or process tank, the cone volume is not dead space — it is working volume for solids. Everything denser than the liquid migrates down the walls and concentrates in the frustum, so the cone acts as a settling zone and drawdown hopper at the same time. The design question is whether the solids leave on their own. At a 60° wall angle, most slurries, yeast and grit slide cleanly to the outlet — this is the self-cleaning threshold behind the brewing, wastewater and silo standards. At 45°, free-flowing material still moves, but sticky or fibrous sludge can bridge and hold, which is why 45° dominates cheap poly tanks (fewer inches of height to mold) while 60° dominates stainless process vessels.

Treat the cone as working volume when you plan capacity, too. A 55-gal cone-bottom tank with 2–3 gal of accumulated sediment effectively stores 52 gal of water above the solids blanket — and each drawdown flush through the 2-in outlet resets it. Sizing the outlet matters here as well: a 2-in drain passes about 4× the solids cross-section of a 1-in stub, which is why hopper bottoms on slurry service step up to 3–4 in even when the liquid flow rate would not need it.

The cone’s gallon figure also sets your drawdown schedule. If the same 55-gal tank collects roughly half a gallon of silt per week, its 7.8-gal cone reaches a full blanket in about 15 weeks — but you should flush long before that, because a blanket deeper than a few inches starts re- entraining solids every time inflow stirs the tank. A quarterly drawdown keeps the settling zone working; an annual one lets the cone become a plugged mass of compacted sediment that no outlet passes. Cheap insurance: mark the calculated dip depth that corresponds to 2 gal of cone contents on the gauge, and flush whenever the reading climbs past it.

❌ Don’t: add the outlet nozzle volume twice. The frustum formula already ends at the outlet diameter — the cone’s bottom face is the nozzle inlet. If your cone height h is measured to the flange face, the frustum includes everything above that face, so only add the nozzle length below it: a 2-in outlet with 4 in of nozzle below the flange adds π × 1² × 4 = 12.6 in³ = 0.05 gal. Counting it inside the frustum and as a stub is a small double-count at 2 in — but the same habit on a 4-in outlet with a 6-in nozzle overcounts by 75 in³ = 0.33 gal, and on multi-tank spreadsheets those errors compound.

Liquid Level to Volume at Any Depth: The Partial Frustum Method

A dip stick reading in a cone-bottom tank translates to volume in two regimes, and the switch happens at the cone height. Here is the method step by step, using the 24-in reference tank (2-in outlet, 6.35-in cone, 36-in cylinder, 75.0 gal total).

Step 1 — datum. Measure depth from the cone tip, the lowest wetted point, not from a floor datum or the outlet flange. A gauge zeroed at the flange reads 2 in high on this tank.

Step 2 — regime test. Is the level ≤ h (inside the cone) or > h (into the cylinder)? The cone/cylinder boundary sits at 6.35 in.

Step 3a — inside the cone, example 3 in. The liquid surface is a circle of radius r_lvl = r + (R − r) × (level ÷ h) = 1 + 11 × (3 ÷ 6.35) = 6.20 in. Then the partial frustum is V = (π × 3 ÷ 3) × (6.20² + 6.20 × 1 + 1²) = 143 in³ = 0.62 gal. For contrast, a flat-bottom cylinder of the same diameter holds π × 144 × 3 = 1,357 in³ = 5.9 gal at the same depth — nearly ten times more. Linear scaling of the total volume is wrong by that same factor in the cone.

Step 3b — above the cone, example 20 in. Add the full cone (1,044 in³) to the cylinder slug: π × 144 × (20 − 6.35) = 6,175 in³. Total 7,219 in³ = 31.25 gal = 41.7% of capacity — the number the calculator’s percent-full bar shows for the default inputs.

For a usable dip chart, run the method at 1-in increments and tabulate the pairs. The cone portion of the chart is curved and dense with detail — 10% of depth is only 0.6% of volume down there — while the cylinder portion is perfectly linear. If you then need to know how long the tank takes to empty through the drain, the tank drain time calculator pairs the level-volume curve with the outlet flow — and the cone segment dominates drain time precisely because the volume per inch collapses toward the tip.

Conical Bottom Tank Volume In Excel

You do not need this page open to run a conical bottom tank volume calculation — a five-cell spreadsheet reproduces it. Lay the inputs down column B with labels in column A, then enter the formulas as chips below. Everything uses ordinary Excel functions: PI(), ^ for powers and IF for the level test.

Copy-friendly layout

A1 / B1Top radius R (in) — 12A2 / B2Cone vertical height h (in) — 6.35A3 / B3Outlet radius r (in) — 1A4 / B4Cylinder height H (in) — 36A5 / B5Liquid level from cone tip (in) — 20

Formulas

B7 — cone gallons (frustum):

=PI()*B2/3*(B1^2+B1*B3+B3^2)/231

B8 — cylinder gallons:

=PI()*B1^2*B4/231

B9 — total gallons, and liters if you want them:

=B7+B8=B9*3.78541

B10 — liquid radius at the level (inside cone or cylinder):

=IF(B5<=B2,B3+(B1-B3)*B5/B2,B1)

B11 — liquid gallons at the level (partial frustum method):

=IF(B5<=B2,PI()*B5/3*(B10^2+B10*B3+B3^2)/231,B7+PI()*B1^2*(B5-B2)/231)

B12 — percent full (format the cell as a percentage):

=B11/B9

Sanity check with the reference tank: B7 returns 4.52, B8 returns 70.50, and with B5 = 20 the level formula returns 31.25 gallons — matching the calculator above. Copy the row down with different B5 values to build a full dip chart in seconds.

Two spreadsheet habits make the sheet durable: anchor the inputs with absolute references (=PI()*$B$2/3*($B$1^2+$B$1*$B$3+$B$3^2)/231) before you fill the formula down a column of level readings, and keep every input in inches so the ÷231 happens in exactly one place. If your tank is all cone and no cylinder, enter 0 in B4 and the cylinder formula simply contributes zero gallons.

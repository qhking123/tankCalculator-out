Aquarium Shape Taxonomy: Which One Do You Have?

Before diving into formulas, the fish tank measurement calculator needs one decision from you: what geometry is the glass trying to hold? Most aquariums fall into three families — rectangular standard tanks, vertical column or hexagon tanks, and stadium or oval tanks — and each uses a different volume formula. Getting this right matters because the error from using the wrong shape formula on a hexagon tank can be a 25–30% overestimate, which means either buying a heater that is too small or a stand that is undersized for the real water weight.

⬜

Rectangular Standard Tank

The most common shape. Length × width × height. Glass panel on every side. Wall thickness is uniform on left/right/front/back and bottom.

V = l × w × h

🟦

Column / Vertical Cylinder

Tall round tube — popular for betta, seahorses, or statement pieces. Glass forms a single cylindrical shell; wall thickness on the circumference is uniform.

V = π × (d/2)² × h

⬭

Oval / Stadium Cross-Section

Hexagon aquariums, bow-front tanks, and custom oval vessels all approximate a stadium — two half-circles joined by a straight rectangle. Area is neither πr² nor lw.

A = π × (d/2) × (w/2)

Step-by-Step Fish Tank Volume Calculation for Each Shape

Once you have identified the shape, you need one more piece of information: is your tank measured on the outside or the inside? Hobbyists buying a second-hand tank almost always measure outside from the box dimensions because the glass is thick and a tape pulled across a corner lies about the true inner dimension. The calculator on this page takes outside dimensions plus glass thickness, subtracts twice the glass from each horizontal dimension and once from height, then runs the formula.

Rectangular Fish Tank — Worked Example

Real-world example: a 29-gallon breeder tank

Outside dimensions: 30″ × 18″ × 18″

Glass thickness: 6 mm (1/4″) ≈ 0.236″

Inside L: 30 − 0.472 = 29.53″

Inside W: 18 − 0.472 = 17.53″

Inside H: 18 − 0.236 = 17.76″

Volume = 29.53 × 17.53 × 17.76

= 9,143 in³

÷ 231 = 39.6 US gal

÷ 61.02 = 149.8 liters

Sticker says 29 gal — that is the nominal nameplate. Geometric capacity of the glass shell is 39.6 gallons. The difference is the 80% fill convention plus freeboard at the top.

Cylinder / Column Tank — Worked Example

Real-world example: a 14″ × 36″ column tank

Outside diameter: 14″

Outside height: 36″

Glass: 4 mm = 0.157″

Inside radius r: (14 − 0.314) / 2 = 6.84″

Inside height h: 36 − 0.157 = 35.84″

V = π × r² × h = π × 6.84² × 35.84

= 52,715 in³

÷ 231 = 228.2 US gal

÷ 61.02 = 864 liters

Oval / Hexagon Tank — Worked Example

Real-world example: a 24″ × 12″ × 18″ hexagon aquarium

Outside length L: 24″

Outside width W (oval diameter): 12″

Glass: 6 mm = 0.236″

Inside area: π × 5.764 × 5.764 = 104.3 in²

Inside height H: 18 − 0.236 = 17.76″

V = area × H = 104.3 × 17.76

= 1,852 in³

÷ 231 = 8.0 US gal

A hexagon treated as a pure rectangle (24 × 12 × 18) gives 21.6 gal — a 170% overestimate that you could fill with an extra 13.6 gallons of water.

Common Mistakes That Break a Fish Tank Volume Calculation

1. Using Outside Dimensions Without Glass Correction

If you pull a tape across a 20-gal long tank and measure 30″ × 12″ × 12″, that is an outside measurement. The glass on each side eats 0.236″ (6 mm) horizontally, so the real inside length is 29.53″ and the width is 11.53″. The geometric capacity from outside dims is 30 × 12 × 12 ÷ 231 =18.7 gal; the real inside capacity is 29.53 × 11.53 × 11.76 ÷ 231 =17.2 gal — an 8% difference. If you use the outside number to pick a heater rated for 20 gallons, the heat exchanger runs at a 9.7% oversize and shortens its life.

2. Treating the Top Rim as Part of the Water Height

Water in a real aquarium does not go to the top. Even an empty tank fills to about 1″ below the rim (for splashing room), and a tank with live plants or a HOB filter needs 2–3″ of freeboard. A 20″ tall tank at full-to-the-brim is 20″ of water; with 1.5″ freeboard that is 18.5″ — a 7.5% reduction. If you calculated the geometric capacity to pick a stand, use full height. If you are ordering fish medicine that says "X ml per 10 gallons," calculate on the real fill height.

3. Applying the Rectangle Formula to a Rounded or Hexagon Tank

The worst offender. A hexagon tank measured outside as 24″ × 14″ × 20″ will give you 28.6 gallons if you treat it as a rectangle. Its true volume, accounting for the hexagon area of roughly 2.598 × (side length)², is 17.9 gallons — a 59% overestimate. That means you would buy a heater rated 60% too large, which will overheat the tank on cool days, shorten its life, and stress the fish.

4. Forgetting Substrate Displacement

Two inches of gravel across the bottom of a 55-gal tank removes roughly 5 gallons of usable water (9%). A heavily planted tank with 3–4″ of aquasoil, driftwood, and rock will remove 8–10 gallons (15%). If you calibrate your dosing pump to a geometric 55 gallons and the real water level is 47 gallons, every fertilizer dose is 17% too strong, which causes algae blooms in planted tanks and salinity crashes in marine tanks.

How to Calculate Fish Tank Weight When Full

Water weighs 8.34 pounds per US gallon, or exactly 1 kilogram per liter. Multiply the real gallons by 8.34 and you get the water weight — but the tank is not just water. A standard glass 55-gal tank shell itself weighs about 60 pounds empty, and the stand, filter, heater, light, and canopy add another 40–80 pounds. A well aquascaped 55-gal tank ready to display sits on your floor at roughly 700–750 pounds — heavier than an adult adult male polar bear.

Floor Load on a Wooden Desk or Stand

A single standard wooden desk rated at 500 pounds will deflect noticeably under a 55-gallon tank — most aquarium enthusiasts learn this lesson by experience. A 55-gal tank on a desk is about 700 pounds on four legs, or 175 pounds per leg. A typical desk leg on carpeted floor distributes roughly 1 square inch of contact, so that is 175 pounds per square inch on the floor joist below. Wood framed floor joists are commonly rated at 40–50 psf dead load, so a 700-pound tank on a 4-foot-long desk puts roughly 175 psf on the joist — more than three times the dead load rating. If you are upstairs, use a purpose-built aquarium stand or put the tank over a load-bearing wall.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate gallons in a fish tank?▼

Measure the inside dimensions of the tank. For a rectangular tank, multiply length × width × height in inches and divide by 231 (the number of cubic inches in one US gallon). For a cylindrical column tank, use the formula V = π × r² × h where r is the inside radius and h is the inside height, then divide by 231. The calculator on this page handles the math for all three aquarium shapes — rectangular, cylindrical, and oval — and converts directly to both gallons and liters.

Does glass wall thickness really affect the fish tank volume calculation?▼

Yes — and more than most hobbyists expect. A standard 10-gallon tank uses about 6 mm (1/4″) glass on every side. That 6 mm on each side takes roughly 5–7% of the geometric capacity of the tank. Premium tanks use 10–12 mm glass, which can eat 10–15% of the sticker volume. The calculator lets you enter the glass thickness so the result matches the water you will actually fill, not the nominal dimensions printed on the box.

How many liters is a 55 gallon fish tank?▼

A 55-gallon tank converts to about 208 liters using the exact factor 1 US gallon = 3.78541 liters. In practice, after subtracting glass wall thickness, a real 55-gal tank holds closer to 200–203 liters of water at full fill, before gravel and decorations are added.

What is the formula for a cylindrical fish tank volume?▼

V = π × (d/2)² × h, where d is the inside diameter and h is the inside height in inches. A 12″ diameter column tank that is 30″ tall has π × 6² × 30 = 3,393 in³ of geometric capacity, or about 14.7 gallons (55.7 liters). Subtract twice the glass thickness from the diameter and once from the height for the real water capacity.

Why does a corner hexagon fish tank not match a pure rectangle formula?▼

A hexagon aquarium has a hexagonal cross-section, and the area of a regular hexagon is (3√3/2) × s² where s is the side length — about 2.598 × s². Treating a hexagon as a rectangle will overstate the area by roughly 30%. The calculator on this page uses an oval approximation for non-rectangular tanks, which covers hexagons, stadium cross-sections, and other rounded shapes with reasonable accuracy.

How much gravel should I account for when filling my tank?▼

Gravel, substrate, rocks, and wood displace water. A typical 55-gal tank with 2″ of gravel across the bottom loses about 5 gallons of usable volume (roughly 9%). A heavily aquascaped tank with 4–6 inches of substrate, driftwood, and rock piles can lose 15–20%. Plan your fill target accordingly — and remember that live rock in a marine tank adds its own weight and displacement, so the final water level may sit 1–2 inches below the nominal full mark.

What is the difference between acrylic and glass fish tank weight calculations?▼

Acrylic tanks use thinner walls for the same volume — 4–5 mm versus 6–12 mm for glass — so the glass wall thickness correction is smaller. Acrylic is lighter than glass, which makes the floor-loading math on wooden furniture less demanding, but the water volume calculation is the same geometric formula either way. The only difference is the wall thickness you enter.

How do I calculate a fish tank that is partially filled?▼

This page's calculator returns total capacity. For a partially filled tank, multiply the total gallons by the percentage of height that is filled — but only if the tank is rectangular or a vertical cylinder. For oval or hexagon tanks the relationship between filled depth and filled volume is not exactly linear because the cross-section changes shape. The main tank volume calculator on the home page handles partial fills of any shape with a dip-chart curve.

Why Gallons and Square Feet Need a Depth Bridge

Gallons and square feet are fundamentally different units. A gallon is volume — three dimensional. A square foot is area — two dimensional. Converting between them is not a single number; it is a family of curves, one for every depth. That is why a pond question like "how many gallons is my 1,000 ft² pond?" always triggers a follow-up: "how deep is it?" The relationship is simple enough once you write it down, and this page automates every direction of it so you never have to do the cubic-foot conversion by hand again.

The core identity is 1 US liquid gallon = 231 cubic inches exactly, which equals 0.133681 cubic feet. Multiplying by 7.48052 turns cubic feet back into gallons. Surface area in square feet times depth in feet gives cubic feet, and the chain closes. Three equations, one magic constant, and every real-world surface-water calculation — from lawn irrigation to koi pond stocking density to rain barrel catchment — resolves itself.

The Three-Way Formula: Gallons ↔ Cubic Feet ↔ Square Feet

Every converter on this page is three formulas sharing one depth variable. Think of depth as the "gear" that slides you between volume and area. Change the depth and all three numbers recompute in real time. Here are the formulas in plain English so you can verify any scene preset with pen and paper:

Cubic feet = square feet × depth_in_feetUS gallons = cubic feet × 7.48052Square feet = gallons ÷ (depth_in_feet × 7.48052)(and depth_in_feet = depth_in_inches ÷ 12)

Let's work the koi pond scene preset by hand to prove it. The preset says 3,000 gallons at 36 inches depth (3 feet). Divide gallons by (depth × 7.48052): 3,000 ÷ (3 × 7.48052) = 3,000 ÷ 22.4416 = 133.7 ft² — which matches the calculator output above when you enter 3,000 gallons and 36 inches. Now reverse it: 1,000 ft² at 18 inches (1.5 feet). Cubic feet = 1,000 × 1.5 = 1,500. Gallons = 1,500 × 7.48052 = 11,220 — the volume of a typical backyard pond, give or take a bog edge.

How to Measure Pond Depth Correctly

The biggest mistake people make when converting gallons to square feet is guessing the depth. "About a foot deep" sounds like a number, but a pond with a shelf at 6 inches and a center at 24 inches averages out to 15 inches — nearly 36% deeper than the guess, which means the real surface area for a given gallon number is 36% smaller. That ripples into stocking density, liner square footage, pump GPH sizing, and how much spring algae you will get away with.

The cross-grid method

Drive four stakes into the ground or the pond walls to form a rectangle that covers the whole water surface. Run a rope down the length and another across the width at every 3–4 foot interval, creating a grid. From each intersection lower a weight on a string until it touches the bottom, measure the wet length of the string, and record it. Average all the numbers. That is your real average depth. A 20-point grid takes about twenty minutes of wading and produces a depth number accurate to within an inch, which is usually good enough.

What if the pond is circular?

Circles have a formula for area — π × r² — so you don't need the grid for the surface area part. But you still need the grid for depth, because a circular pond with a center drain is still deeper in the middle than at the edges. Once you have the average depth, multiply the area by depth in feet to get cubic feet, then × 7.48052 for gallons. Going the other direction — gallons to diameter — divide gallons by (depth × 7.48052) to get cubic feet, divide by π to get radius squared, and take the square root.

Cubic Feet — The Honest Middleman

Engineers and landscapers secretly prefer cubic feet over gallons because it is the same unit on both sides of the volume-area bridge. One cubic foot of water weighs about 62.4 pounds (the weight you use to calculate foundation load), and it is one foot times one foot times one foot of depth — so the conversion to square feet is literally divide by depth in feet, no 7.48 factor needed. The three-way calculator on this page keeps cubic feet visible in the result panel exactly so you can see that middle step and sanity-check the gallons output. If you ever have a gallons-vs-liters brain fart, glance at cubic feet and do the division yourself — you will catch an entry mistake faster than any decimal place on the gallon number.

Real-World Use Cases That Hit This Calculator Daily

1. Sprinkler system design

A sprinkler head typically flows 2–4 gallons per minute. To size the pump and the zones, you need to know how many minutes each zone runs to deliver your target watering depth. If your front lawn is 1,200 ft² and you want 1/2 inch per cycle, the comparison table above tells you 0.667 gal/ft², so total gallons per cycle = 1,200 × 0.667 = 800 gal. A 4 GPM zone would run 200 minutes per cycle — way too long. Split into two zones, 100 minutes each, and you are in business.

2. Koi pond stocking density

The widely cited koi stocking rule is 1 inch of koi per 10 gallons, but a lot of hobbyists apply it without realizing the density should really be tied to surface area for oxygen exchange. A 3 ft deep pond has a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio than a 6 ft deep pond of the same gallons, so you can actually carry slightly more fish. Convert gallons back to square feet at your measured depth to get the true footprint, then plan your koi count accordingly.

3. Rain barrel catchment sizing

The standard rule of thumb is 1 inch of rain on 1,000 ft² of roof catches 623 gallons. That number comes from the exact formula on this page — 1,000 ft² × 1/12 ft depth = 83.33 ft³, × 7.48052 = 623 gal. If your roof is 2,500 ft² and you want to store 500 gallons, you need to capture about 0.32 inches of rain — which is roughly what falls in a light summer shower in most temperate climates.

4. Pool liner square footage

A pool liner is quoted in square feet. For a rectangular pool the formula is simple — twice the perimeter times the average depth, plus the bottom. But if you only know the pool's volume in gallons (from the builder brochure), the three-way calculator in mode B will take the gallons and the average depth and return the surface area of the bottom directly. Add the wall area manually and you have your liner order number.

The Four Things You Should Never Do With This Conversion

1. Never omit the depth

"How many square feet in 1,000 gallons?" is mathematically unanswerable. A 1,000-gallon pond at 6 inches deep is 2,671 ft². At 3 feet deep it is only 445 ft². Same gallons, six times less surface. If a calculator ever gives you a one-number answer without asking for depth, it is either wrong or it has silently assumed a depth — usually 1 foot — and not told you.

2. Never mix US and imperial gallons

This page uses US gallons (7.48052 per cubic foot). Imperial gallons (6.22883 per cubic foot) are about 20% bigger in cubic inches, so mixing them introduces an automatic 20% error. If your pump label is printed in imperial gallons (common on UK and some Australian gear), multiply by 1.2 to convert to US gallons before entering.

3. Never ignore the dished bottom

Ponds almost never have flat bottoms. Even a well-built in-ground pool has a slope to the drain. The "average depth" from a few center-point measurements will overstate the volume because most of the water is concentrated in the narrowest deep section. Use the grid method described above, split into depth zones, or at minimum measure the shallow and deep ends and average them — do not just drop a tape in one spot and call it done.

4. Never assume full-to-the-brim

Ponds, pools and tanks are rarely filled to the absolute rim. There is freeboard (1–3 inches below the lip on purpose to catch spray), overflow level, and in pools a skimmer line that sets the working water level. If you calculated 1,000 gallons full-to-the-brim, expect the real working volume to be 940–980 gallons. That 2–6% difference is small enough to ignore for stocking density but big enough to matter for dosing water treatments that say "X ml per 10 gallons."

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert gallons to square feet?▼

You cannot convert gallons directly to square feet without a depth assumption. A gallon is a unit of volume and a square foot is a unit of area — depth is the missing dimension that bridges them. The formula is: square feet = gallons / (depth_in_feet × 7.48052). If you know the average depth of your pond or pool, enter it in the calculator and it will solve for the surface area. If you only know the gallons, pick the depth preset that matches your project (shallow lawn, medium pond, deep pool) to get the implied square footage.

How many square feet does one gallon of water cover?▼

One US gallon of water spread to a uniform 1 inch depth covers exactly 16.04 square feet. That is the number 1 / (1/12) / 7.48052 = 12 / 7.48052 = 1.604 ft² per gallon at 1 inch — wait, no: 1 gallon = 231 cubic inches, so 231 / 1 inch depth = 231 square inches, and 231 / 144 = 1.604 square feet at 1 inch. At 6 inches depth (shallow irrigation), one gallon covers 0.267 ft². At 3 feet depth (koi pond), one gallon covers only 0.022 ft². The calculator on this page uses the exact cubic-inch definition so the number is always right.

How many gallons is a 10 x 10 foot pool?▼

A 10 ft × 10 ft pool that is 3 feet deep holds 10 × 10 × 3 = 300 cubic feet. Multiply by 7.48052 gallons per cubic foot and you get 2,244 gallons. If the pool has a gradual slope from 3 ft to 5 ft, use the average depth (4 ft) so the total is 10 × 10 × 4 × 7.48052 = 2,992 gallons. In practice, in-ground pools with a sloped bottom should use average depth for the entire basin — measuring the deepest and shallowest points and dividing by two.

How many gallons per square foot for irrigation?▼

The standard answer in US landscape contracting is 1 gallon per square foot per application — which equals 0.0625 inches of water per event. That number comes from 1 gal = 231 in³ divided by 144 in² in a square foot, giving 1.604 inches depth per gallon. Landscape pros divide by about 25 to get the real watering depth once the soil absorbs and evaporates half, so they target roughly 0.0625 in per session. If you are calculating the output of a sprinkler system, divide total gallons per minute by the square footage covered to get gallons per square foot.

Why can't I use a flat formula when the pond bottom is irregular?▼

Because square feet of surface area times an average depth only works if the pond is a rectangular prism, and almost no real pond is. Koi ponds have sloped walls, a bottom drain pit, and sometimes a bog edge that is barely 6 inches deep. If you blindly divide total gallons by 3 ft depth you overstate the surface area of the deep hole and understate the surface area of the shallow shelf, which throws off your stocking density, pump sizing, and liner estimate. The remedy is to split the pond into depth zones, calculate gallons per zone, and sum them — the three-way calculator on this page supports that by letting you add zone volumes together.

What is the difference between US gallons and imperial gallons for this conversion?▼

This calculator uses US liquid gallons throughout because the vast majority of pond, pool, and irrigation products sold in the United States, Canada, and Australia quote US gallons. The conversion factor is 7.48052 US gal per cubic foot. If you need imperial gallons (mostly used in the UK for very large water features), the factor is 6.22883 imperial gal per cubic foot — about 16% less. If your pool pump label says '15,000 imperial gal,' multiply by 1.2 to get the US-gallon equivalent before entering it here.

How do I calculate gallons to square feet for a circular pond?▼

First get the surface area of the circle: A = π × r² where r is the radius in feet. Then multiply by average depth in feet to get cubic feet, then multiply by 7.48052 to get gallons. To go the other direction (gallons back to diameter), divide gallons by (depth × 7.48052) to get cubic feet, then divide by π to get radius squared, and take the square root. The three-way calculator on this page can take it from there — you just need to know the radius and average depth.

How much does water weigh per gallon vs per square foot?▼

One US gallon of fresh water at room temperature weighs 8.34 pounds. That is a volume-to-weight number, not an area number. To get weight per square foot you need depth: a 1 ft² column of water that is 1 ft deep weighs 62.4 pounds (the density of water in lb per cubic foot). One gallon on a square foot at 1 inch depth is 8.34 lb ÷ 12 = 0.695 lb per square foot. That matters when you calculate the bearing on a concrete patio — a 200 ft² pond with 1 foot depth weighs 200 × 62.4 = 12,480 lb, and your deck or patio foundation must carry that plus the liner, rocks, and fish.

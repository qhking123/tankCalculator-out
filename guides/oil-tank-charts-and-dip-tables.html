Horizontal vs Vertical — Same Volume, Different Curves

The orientation of a tank dramatically changes its dip chart. Two tanks with identical capacity — one lying down, one standing up — produce completely different relationships between inches of depth and gallons of fuel. This is why a 25% dipstick reading means 19.6% volume on a horizontal tank but exactly 25% on a vertical one.

💡 Visual proof: On a horizontal cylinder, the cross-section is a circle. Picture the circle's top half — at the very bottom (25% down), the chord is thin, holding only a crescent. Exactly halfway (50% depth), the chord slices the circle perfectly in two — 50% volume. A vertical tank cuts the circle straight across at every height, so each inch adds the same gallons. If you need a linear dip chart for inventory management, vertical tanks are the tool — and the vertical cylinder tank volume calculator gives you the linear gallons-per-inch constant instantly.

💡 Read straight down to the bottom shell — not to the fill port

On a horizontal tank the gauge stick must be lowered until it touches the actual bottom shell — the plate the fuel sits on. If you measure to the fill port (which is almost always offset vertically by 2–4 inches), your reading will be off by exactly that amount. The dip chart expects a measurement from the true bottom shell. One edge case: if the tank is deliberately installed with a 1–2° slope toward the drain, correct for tilt using the tilt feature on the home page calculator.

⚠️ Always subtract the dead space and dip tube

Your system cannot draw fuel from the very bottom of the tank. A typical heating-oil tank has a bottom outlet 1–1.5 inches above the bottom shell — any fuel below that level sits as dead weight you cannot reach. Propane tanks have a fixed dip tube stamping on the nameplate; on a 500 gallon bullet that tube is typically 8 inches above the bottom. Subtract this fixed amount from every dip reading before using any chart, including this guide's. For regulatory delivery and storage readings, this is not optional.

❌ Don't divide total gallons by inches of diameter

If you take a 275 gal tank (27 inches diameter) and divide 275 ÷ 27 ≈ 10.2 gallons per inch, you will be wrong across almost all of the tank. That number only works at exactly mid-depth. Near the bottom and top of the tank each inch holds far fewer gallons, and near mid-height each inch holds more. The circular segment math that produces the real dip chart is non-linear for exactly this reason. Any shortcut calculation will give you a roughly correct estimate that can be off by 20–25% near the extremes of the tank.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my tank's actual dip chart different from the one in this guide — even for the same gallon rating?▼

Tanks of the same nominal gallon rating can have different exact dimensions. A '275 gal' heating-oil tank from one brand might measure 58 × 44 × 27 inches while another is 60 × 42 × 28 inches. Both round to 275 gallons on the sticker but their dip charts differ by several gallons at key readings. The charts in this guide use the most common industry dimensions for each class. For a certified dip chart specific to your tank, you need either a professional strapping service (they physically measure the tank's volume at each inch of depth) or to enter your exact inside dimensions into the dip chart generator on the calculator home page.

How do I dip the tank correctly — where do I measure from, and what do I subtract?▼

Lower a clean gauge stick straight down until it touches the bottom of the tank. Mark the level of liquid on the stick with a glove or your thumbnail, pull it out, and read the wet depth in inches. For horizontal tanks, measure straight down from the top shell to the bottom shell — not from the fill port, which is usually offset. Read the inches, then look up gallons in the dip chart. Before any delivery or tax-reporting dip reading, subtract the 'dead space' — the bottom few inches where the outlet pipe sits. For most heating-oil tanks this is about 1–1.5 inches. For propane tanks, subtract the fixed dip tube extension (usually stamped on the tank) because the tube opens above the bottom, leaving liquid below it that the system cannot draw.

Why does a horizontal tank dip chart show that 25% depth is only about 19.6% volume?▼

Because the horizontal tank's cross-section is a circle, and circles are narrow at the bottom. At exactly a quarter of the diameter (25% depth), the chord slices off only the thin crescent-shaped segment at the very bottom of the circle. That crescent occupies only about 19.6% of the full circle's area. The formula: segment area A = r² × arccos((r-h)/r) - (r-h) × √(2rh-h²). For h = 0.25D (quarter depth), A/circle_area ≈ 0.196. By symmetry, three-quarters depth holds about 80% of capacity. Only at exactly half depth — where the circle is perfectly symmetric — does depth equal volume. This is the fundamental reason you cannot divide total gallons by inches of diameter to make a straight-line dip chart.

What is the difference between a dip chart, a strapping table, and a calibration table?▼

They are the same document. Dip chart and strapping table are the two most common field names — 'strapping' comes from the old practice of physically measuring (strapping) the tank at each inch of depth with a surveyor's tape and converting the measured segment area to volume. Calibration table is the more formal engineering name, especially when certified for custody transfer (like fuel deliveries). All three terms describe a depth-to-volume lookup table, usually in 1-inch increments, used to convert a gauge-stick reading to gallons. The free dip chart generator on the home page produces a mathematically computed version of this document; certified charts require physical measurement by a licensed tank calibration service.

Why does a vertical tank have a linear dip chart while a horizontal one is non-linear?▼

Because a vertical cylinder has the same circular cross-section at every height. Fill one inch more and you add exactly πr² × 1 inch of volume — always the same amount. Depth and volume are directly proportional, so 25% depth = 25% gallons, 50% = 50%, and so on. This makes vertical tanks ideal for accurate inventory by dipstick — you can even use a simple gallons-per-inch constant. A horizontal cylinder has a circular cross-section running across the diameter instead of along the axis, so the cross-section's filled area changes non-linearly with depth (narrow at the bottom, widest in the middle, narrow again at the top). The vertical tank is the only common shape with a perfectly linear dip chart.

Can I use a dip chart from a 330 gal tank on my 300 gal tank — they look almost the same?▼

No — even a small difference in dimensions creates a meaningful gap. A 330 gal tank might be 27 inches in diameter and 72 inches long, while a '300 gal' from another source is 26 inches diameter × 70 inches long. At quarter depth the difference between the two tanks' actual filled volume can be 5–8 gallons. If you are estimating, the nearest chart gets you in the ballpark. If you are measuring for a delivery, billing, or regulatory purposes, you need the exact dimensions. Measure the tank's inside diameter/length/height, enter them into the dip chart generator, and use that — it takes 10 seconds and eliminates the guess.

Case Study: The 275 Gallon Oval Heating Oil Tank

The 275-gallon oval is the default home heating oil tank across the northern United States and eastern Canada. If you have oil heat and your tank sits outside beside the house, odds are very good it is a 275-gallon oval. Understanding this one tank is understanding 80% of all home heating oil tank questions. Let's walk through its geometry from the sticker to the usable gallons you actually burn.

275 gal oval tank — the numbers, step by step

Nameplate: 275 gallons — stamped on the tank's end cap or wall panel

Real dimensions: 60" long × 44" wide × 27" tall (oval cross-section)

Geometric interior volume: ~268 gallons (calculated from the oval area × length, minus wall thickness — the nameplate rounds up to a clean number)

90% fill rule applied: 268 × 0.90 ≈ 241 gallons max safe fill

Bottom 5% sludge/water reserve: the lowest 5% of the tank's volume is never burned — water settles, sludge accumulates, and you want it nowhere near your fuel filter or injector

Final usable capacity: approximately 240 gallons

The path from 275 sticker gallons to 240 usable gallons has three gates. Gate one is the manufacturer's rounding: 268 gallons of real interior volume gets marketed as 275 for round number appeal — this varies by tank maker but is typically 2–3%. Gate two is the 90% fill rule: every heating oil tank, like every pressurized liquid vessel, needs headspace for agitation during delivery, minor temperature swings, and the small amount of foaming that happens when oil hits the tank. Heating oil expands far less than propane (0.07% per 10°F vs. 1.5% per 10°F for propane), so the industry settled on 90% fill instead of the 80% rule propane lives under. Gate three is the bottom reserve: the lowest few inches of every heating oil tank are where water (heavier than oil) and sludge settle. You never dip low enough to pull from this zone.

This 240-gallon usable figure is the number every professional uses. It is what the delivery driver programs into the truck, it is what your dip chart is designed around, and it is what your heating cost estimates should assume. If you take away one number from this page, make it this one: a 275-gallon home heating oil tank delivers 240 usable gallons.

Usable Capacity vs. Nameplate: Why the Numbers Never Match

The sticker says one number. The dip chart says another. The delivery driver uses a third. This mismatch is the single biggest source of confusion for heating oil homeowners, and it is completely predictable once you know the three adjustments applied to every tank size.

The Three Adjustments

1. Geometric rounding. Tank manufacturers calculate the true interior volume from sheet-metal dimensions — wall thicknesses, corner radii, end caps — and then round up to a marketable number. A tank with 268 gallons of real interior volume gets sold as "275 gallons." This is usually a 2–3% difference.

2. The 90% fill rule. Unlike propane's 80% limit, heating oil tanks are filled to 90% of their geometric volume. The reason is thermal expansion. Heating oil is far less temperature-sensitive than liquid propane: it expands about 0.07% per 10°F temperature increase, compared to propane's 1.5%. That is a 21× difference. On a 50°F warm-up, a 275-gal tank's oil expands by roughly 0.35% — less than a gallon. The 10% headspace handles agitation, foam, and any spill risk during delivery, but it is not there to absorb massive thermal expansion the way propane's ullage is.

3. Bottom sludge/water reserve. Every heating oil tank accumulates water at the bottom. Water is denser than No. 2 fuel oil, so it settles out of suspension over time — from condensation inside the tank, from minor amounts dissolved in the fuel itself, or from leaks if the tank is old. Below the water sits sludge: heavy hydrocarbons, rust particles, and contaminants that settled out. This material is never pumped out during a delivery, and you never want to run your furnace on it — it clogs filters, gums injectors, and burns with a smoky flame. The bottom 5% of the tank is therefore treated as permanently unavailable. Combined with the 90% fill rule, this gives us the simple industry formula: usable ≈ nameplate × 0.90.

On a cylindrical or oval tank, that is completely wrong. The cross-section is narrow at the bottom (and narrow at the top), so the volume per inch of depth is highest in the middle and lowest at the extremes. For an oval heating oil tank:

Delivery Math: Exactly How Much Oil to Order

Once you have your usable capacity and your current dip reading, the delivery math could not be simpler:

gallons to order = usable capacity − current dip reading

Let's run the worked example that powers the mini calculator on this page. Your tank is the standard 275-gallon oval, so usable capacity is 240 gallons. You lower the dip stick, pull it out, and the wet mark corresponds to 60 gallons on your dip chart. How much to order?

Worked example — 275-gal tank, dip reads 60 gal

Usable capacity of 275-gal tank: 240 gal

Current dip reading: 60 gal

Gallons to top up: 240 − 60 = 180 gallons

The delivery truck's meter will show 180 gallons delivered. After the fill, your dip should read very close to the 240-gal usable line on your chart — do not expect the geometric 268 or the nameplate 275.

A few rules of thumb for delivery planning. Do not let your tank go below 1/4 usable — that is where the bottom reserve starts getting risky and you can pull water into the pump. A full delivery truck typically carries 3,000–6,000 gallons, so if you order 180 gallons (our worked example above) you are just a small stop on the driver's route — expect a quick in-and-out visit. If you have two tanks or a larger home with 500+ gallons of usable, you might get a full 300–400 gallon delivery, which takes longer. And always order the same company on a regular schedule — they will know your tank, bring your favorite fuel blend (winter vs. summer), and are far less likely to make a meter-reading error than a one-off driver.

Why Heating Oil Uses a 90% Fill Rule (and Propane Uses 80%)

Every pressurized liquid vessel needs headspace. But "how much" depends entirely on how much the liquid inside expands when warmed. That single physical property — thermal expansion coefficient — is the reason propane tanks stop at 80% and heating oil tanks run to 90%.

Propane's story: the 1.5% problem

Liquid propane expands approximately 1.5% in volume for every 10°F temperature rise. A 500-gallon tank filled to 100% on a 40°F spring morning would have grown to 537.5 gallons by 100°F in the afternoon — 37.5 gallons more than the tank can hold. Without headspace, that liquid would have nowhere to go except to push open the pressure relief valve and vent vapor, or in the worst case, rupture the tank. The 20% ullage on propane tanks absorbs all that expansion. This is mandated by NFPA 58 and enforced by DOT regulations — every certified propane driver knows to stop at 80%.

Heating oil's story: the 0.07% reality

Heating oil (No. 2 fuel oil) is dramatically less sensitive to temperature — it expands only about 0.07% for every 10°F rise. On that same 50°F swing, a 275-gallon tank's oil expands by roughly 0.35%, or less than one gallon. The 10% headspace on a heating oil tank is therefore not about thermal expansion at all. It is about:

  • Delivery agitation. When a truck pumps oil in, it hits the tank bottom at high velocity. The headspace lets the liquid slosh without overflowing.
  • Foam. Heating oil traps tiny bubbles when agitated, creating temporary volume the dip chart would otherwise misread.
  • Operator safety margin. Even a careful driver can miss the dip mark by an inch or two. The 10% buffer means that mistake costs a few gallons of under-fill, not a fuel spill on your driveway.
  • Vent clearance. Every tank has a vent pipe near the top. Filling into the vent zone risks pushing oil out through the roof or wall vent — a messy and expensive clean-up.

The bottom 5% reserve on heating oil tanks has no analog on propane tanks, and that is a combustion-quality reason, not a safety reason. Water and sludge settle out of heating oil and collect at the bottom. Propane, being a liquefied gas under pressure, boils the moment pressure is released — it cannot hold water or sludge in solution the way liquid oil can. That is why your propane tank has no bottom drain valve, but your heating oil tank does.

Frequently Asked Questions About Heating Oil Tanks

Why does a 275 gallon heating oil tank only hold 240 gallons of usable fuel?▼

Three things eat into the 275-gallon nameplate. First, the geometric interior volume of a typical 275-gal oval tank (60" L × 44" W × 27" H) is about 268 gallons — the nameplate rounds up. Second, the 90% fill rule: heating oil expands less than propane (roughly 0.07% per 10°F vs. propane's 1.5%), so the industry allows 90% fill instead of 80%, but that still leaves 10% headspace. Third, the bottom 5% of the tank is reserved for water accumulation and sludge that settles out of heating oil — you never want to burn that, because it clogs filters and injectors. The math: 275 × 0.90 = 247.5, minus the geometric rounding gives 240 usable gallons. This is the number every delivery driver, HVAC tech, and home heat calculator uses.

What exactly is a "quarter tank" on a heating oil gauge?▼

This is the most common mistake homeowners make. On a rectangular tank, a quarter of the depth means a quarter of the volume. On a typical horizontal oval heating oil tank, that is not true — the oval is narrow at the bottom. At 25% of the tank's vertical height, a 275-gal oval holds only about 20% of its total volume, or roughly 55 gallons, not 69 gallons (which would be one-quarter of 275). Conversely, at 75% depth it holds about 80% of the volume. This is why you should never judge a heating oil tank by depth alone — you need a dip chart, which maps each inch of stick reading to the actual gallons in the tank. Our mini calculator on this page shows the depth-to-volume ratio in real time.

How do I read a heating oil dip chart?▼

A dip chart is a simple two-column table that maps depth (inches from the bottom of the tank) to gallons. For a 275-gal oval tank, it looks roughly like this: 0" = 0 gal, 5" = 30 gal, 10" = 75 gal, 13.5" (quarter depth) = 55 gal (not ~69 gal!), 20" = 200 gal, 27" (full depth) = 268 geometric gal. To use it: lower a clean stick until it touches the bottom, pull it straight up, read the wet mark in inches, and look up that row on the chart. That is your current gallons. Subtract from your usable capacity (240 gal for a 275-gal tank) to get the delivery quantity. Every heating oil company will happily give you a laminated dip chart for your tank model, or you can generate one with the main tank volume calculator.

Heating oil vs. propane fill rule — why is heating oil 90% and propane 80%?▼

The difference comes down to thermal expansion. Liquid propane expands roughly 1.5% in volume for every 10°F temperature rise. Heating oil expands only about 0.07% over the same range — about one-twenty-first as much. Propane needs the extra 20% ullage because even a 50°F warm-up would overflow a 100% full tank. Heating oil can safely operate at 90% fill because its expansion is negligible over normal temperature swings. The remaining 10% headspace on a heating oil tank is there for agitation during delivery, minor slosh, and a safety margin — not because the liquid would otherwise expand out. The bottom 5% of a heating oil tank is additionally reserved for water and sludge, which is a combustion-quality concern rather than a pressure concern.

How much heating oil should I order to top up my tank?▼

The delivery math is simple once you know your tank's usable capacity: gallons to top up = usable capacity − current dip reading. For a 275-gal tank (240 usable) that currently reads 60 gallons on the dip stick, you need 240 − 60 = 180 gallons. The delivery truck will add exactly that amount. You never order to the nameplate capacity — you order to usable, because the last 5–10 gallons at the very top are where the vent pipe is and you risk overflowing, and the bottom 5% is reserve. How much does heating oil weigh per gallon?▼

Heating oil (No. 2 fuel oil, the standard home-heating grade) weighs approximately 6.96 pounds per US gallon at 60°F. Water weighs 8.34 lb/gal, so heating oil is about 83% the weight of water. This matters when you are estimating how heavy a full tank is — a 240-gallon usable fill of heating oil adds about 1,670 pounds to the tank, on top of the empty steel shell (typically 300–400 lb for a 275-gal oval). If you are using a scale to verify a delivery, weigh the truck before and after, divide the net weight by 6.96, and you get gallons delivered. Density varies slightly with temperature and grade (winter blend vs. summer blend), but 6.96 lb/gal is the industry standard number.

Is 275 gallons the right size for my home?▼

For most single-family homes in the northern United States, 275 gallons is the default — it is sized to deliver roughly 2–3 weeks of peak heating between deliveries, which is the typical service cycle. A 275-gal tank covers 1,500–2,500 square feet in Zone 4 (US climate map: Midwest, Northeast). Homes larger than 2,500 ft² or in colder climates (Zone 5–7) typically step up to a 330-gal or 500-gal tank. The rule of thumb is 100 gallons of usable heating oil per 1,000 ft² per heating season — but you should also plan for at least 7–10 days of peak demand at a time, since you do not want to run out during a storm.

Methods & Formulas

How Tank Volume Is Calculated, Shape by Shape

The methods below compute cubic measures from the dimensions you enter; the calculator then converts cubic feet to gallons and cubic meters to liters automatically. Total capacity follows the solid geometry of each shape. Filled volume is a separate calculation — for anything with a circular cross-section lying on its side, the wetted area is a circular segment, and the relationship between depth and volume is genuinely non-linear. Used together, the cards below turn this page into a complete cylindrical and rectangular tank volume calculator: pick the shape above and each formula shows exactly what the engine is doing.

Horizontal Cylinder Tank

Total volume is the circular end area times the length: V(tank) = πr²l. The filled volume uses the area of the circular segment: A = ½r²(θ − sin θ), where θ = 2·arccos(m/r) and m is the distance from the surface to the centerline. If the fill height f is below half the diameter, the fill is the segment; above the halfway line the empty segment is subtracted from the total instead.

This is the shape of fuel-oil tanks, propane bullets and road tankers — and the reason a quarter-depth reading is not a quarter of the volume.

Vertical Cylinder Tank

V(tank) = πr²h, and the filled volume is simply a shorter cylinder of the same radius: V(fill) = πr²f. Depth and volume are perfectly linear — every inch of depth holds the same gallons, which is why upright rainwater tanks are the easiest to gauge by stick.

Rectangle Tank (Volume of a Rectangular Tank)

A rectangular prism: V(tank) = l·w·h, and the fill is V(fill) = l·w·f. Aquariums, cisterns and IBCs are linear like the vertical cylinder — one cubic foot holds 7.48052 US gallons, so a 48 × 24 × 12 inch box is 13,104 in³ ≈ 56.7 gallons. In metric, the volume of a rectangular tank in litres is just length × width × height in centimetres ÷ 1,000 — a 120 × 50 × 60 cm box holds exactly 360 L.

Horizontal Oval Tank

The end is a stadium — two half-circles joined by a rectangle — with r = h/2 and a = w − h (w > h). Total: V(tank) = (πr² + 2ra)·l. The fill is computed as a horizontal cylinder of diameter h plus a rectangle of width a: V(fill) = V(segment) + V(rectangle). This is the classic road fuel-transport cross-section.

Vertical Oval Tank

Same stadium end but standing upright (h > w), with r = w/2 and a = h − w. Filling crosses three regimes: below the bottom semicircle the segment formula applies; between the semicircles the fill is exactly half the cylinder section plus the rectangular middle; near the top the empty segment is subtracted from the total.

Capsule Tanks (horizontal & vertical)

A capsule is a sphere split in half and separated by a cylinder of diameter d and straight length a: V = πr²(4r/3 + a). Horizontal capsules fill by the circular-segment method in the body plus the spherical-cap volume V(cap) = ⅓πh²(3R − h) in the heads; vertical capsules move through three regimes exactly like the vertical oval.

Horizontal Ellipse Tank

A true elliptical cross-section with semi-axes a = w/2 and b = h/2: V(tank) = π·a·b·l. Because an ellipse is a circle stretched horizontally, the segment area scales by a/b — the fill is the circle segment of radius b multiplied by the stretch factor.

2:1 Elliptical & Dish-Ended Tanks

These are horizontal cylinders whose heads bulge outward. A 2:1 semi-elliptical head (depth D/4) adds πr³/3 per head; a dished-only (ASME F&D style) head adds roughly 0.0809·D³. The calculator reports the head contribution separately from the shell so you can see exactly what the ends are worth — often several gallons on a small vessel.

Round Water Tank Capacity Calculation Formula

Household and farm water storage almost always uses a round tank — an upright cylinder — and its capacity formula is the simplest on this page: V = π × r² × h, with r the inside radius and h the water height. A round water tank 1.8 m across and 2 m tall therefore holds π × 0.9² × 2 = 5.09 m³ — about 5,090 litres or 1,345 US gallons. Working in inches, a 48-inch diameter, 60-inch tall round tank holds π × 24² × 60 = 108,573 in³ ≈ 470 US gallons ≈ 1,779 litres. A round tank lying on its side is the exception: depth-to-volume stops being linear, so use the horizontal cylinder method above and the dip chart instead of the one-line formula.

⚙️ Dish Ends & Tank Heads

Why Head Type Changes the Capacity

Most hand calculators assume flat ends, but industrial vessels are built with curved heads that add real capacity — on a 36-inch diameter vessel the difference between flat and hemispherical heads is over 26 gallons, more than 10% of the shell volume. Tips for accurate measurements

  • Measure the inside dimensions — capacity is defined by the wetted space, not the shell.
  • If you can only measure outside, subtract twice the wall thickness from each dimension before calculating.
  • Keep each dimension's unit selector consistent — mixing inches and centimeters silently skews the result.
  • For dosing, mixing or deliveries, base your numbers on the measured water line, not the nameplate or brimful capacity.

Tank Volume Calculator FAQ

How do I calculate the volume of a tank?▼

Measure the inside dimensions of the tank, pick the shape, and apply the matching formula. A vertical cylinder is π × r² × height; a rectangle is length × width × height; a horizontal cylinder is π × r² × length. This calculator does the geometry for you and converts the result into US gallons, imperial gallons, liters, cubic feet and cubic meters in one step — and it handles the hard cases (partial fill, dished heads, tilt) that hand formulas ignore.

How many gallons does a 275-gallon oil tank actually hold?▼

A standard 275-gallon home heating-oil tank measures roughly 60" long with a 27 × 44-inch oval (stadium) cross-section. Its geometric capacity works out to about 268 US gallons — the nameplate rounds up. Because the usable fill stops at about 90% for expansion, expect roughly 240 usable gallons, which is why a "quarter-tank" reading matters more than the sticker.

Why is a quarter-depth dipstick reading not a quarter of the volume?▼

A horizontal tank's circular cross-section is narrow at the bottom, widest at the middle and narrow again at the top. At exactly half depth the tank is 50% full by symmetry — but at a quarter depth it typically holds only 19–20% of capacity, and at three-quarters depth about 80%. Volume follows a non-linear curve, which is precisely what the dip chart below tabulates.

What is a dip chart or tank gauge table?▼

A dip chart (also called a strapping or calibration table) lists liquid depth readings against the volume they represent, from empty to full. Field staff dip the tank with a gauge stick, read the wet depth, and look up the volume instantly — no math on site. Set an increment (every inch, centimeter or 5% of depth) and the generator above prints the full table, with the filled volume, percentage and remaining ullage for each row.

Should I measure the tank's inside or outside dimensions?▼

Inside dimensions. The calculator assumes the numbers you enter are internal measurements — capacity is defined by the wetted space. If you can only measure the outside, subtract twice the wall thickness from each dimension (and remember that steel tanks commonly lose a little more to internal corrosion scale over their life). A half-inch of wall on a 36-inch diameter tank overstates capacity by about 5%.

What is the difference between flat, hemispherical and 2:1 elliptical heads?▼

Flat heads are simple closing plates and add no volume. Hemispherical heads are half-spheres (depth = D/2) and add the most: together they contribute a full sphere's worth of volume. 2:1 elliptical heads are half-ellipses with depth = D/4 — the ASME pressure-vessel standard — and add exactly half of what hemispherical heads do. Torispherical (dished, ASME F&D style) heads are shallower still and add roughly 0.081 × D³ per head. The head panel above reports the head volume separately from the body.

How much does the liquid in my tank weigh?▼

Pick the liquid and the calculator multiplies the volume by its density. Water weighs 8.35 lb per US gallon (1 kg per liter), diesel about 7.1 lb/gal, gasoline about 6.2 lb/gal and milk about 8.6 lb/gal. A 275-gallon tank of heating oil (SG ≈ 0.85) holds roughly 1,950 lb — close to a ton — which matters for stands, pads, floors and delivery trucks.

Does tank tilt really change the volume reading?▼

Yes — on horizontal tanks it can. Installers deliberately slope tanks toward the drain end, and even a 1–2° tilt shifts the depth-to-volume relationship noticeably near the ends of the range. At 2° over a 60-inch tank the ends differ by more than two inches of liquid depth. Enable the tilt correction above, enter the angle (or the end-to-end drop) and where you took the dip reading, and the engine integrates the depth along the whole length instead of assuming a level tank.

How do I convert cubic feet to gallons?▼

One cubic foot holds 7.48052 US gallons (and 28.317 liters), so multiply cubic feet by 7.48052. Going the other way, divide gallons by 7.48052. For oil barrels, one 42-gallon barrel is 5.615 cubic feet. The results panel shows every volume unit at once, so no manual conversion is needed.

What is ullage?▼

Ullage is the unfilled space above the liquid in a tank — the room left for thermal expansion, vapour and sloshing. Fuel deliveries typically stop around 90–95% full to preserve it. The results panel reports ullage in every volume unit, and the dip chart's final column tracks it at every depth so you can see exactly how much more the tank can take at any reading.

What is the difference between tank volume and tank capacity?▼

Volume is the pure geometric space inside the tank — what the formulas compute from your dimensions. Capacity is what the tank can usefully hold in practice, which is often lower: freeboard at the top, internal baffles, fittings, pumps and the manufacturer's fill limit all eat into it. When comparing tanks for a project, always check whether a published figure refers to total geometric volume or rated (usable) capacity, and confirm which one your local code requires.

How do I calculate tank volume in liters?▼

Measure in centimeters and the product of your dimensions is cubic centimeters: divide by 1,000 for liters. If you measure in meters, multiply cubic meters by 1,000. Working in inches, divide cubic inches by 61.024 to get liters; working in feet, multiply cubic feet by 28.317. The converter section below does all of these conversions live, and the results panel shows liters alongside US and imperial gallons automatically.

How do I size a septic tank or chemical storage tank?▼

The geometry is identical to any other tank — enter the internal dimensions and the calculator returns the volume regardless of what the tank holds. What differs is how the required size is determined. Septic tank sizing is set by your local health department based on occupancy and wastewater flow, and chemical storage depends on the chemical's properties, secondary containment and applicable codes. Use the calculator to convert proposed dimensions into a volume figure, then confirm that figure against the authority having jurisdiction or a qualified engineer before purchasing.

How full can I fill a propane tank?▼

Never more than 80% of its water capacity — this is a legal requirement, not a suggestion. Liquid propane expands roughly 1.5% for every 10 °F of temperature rise, and the 20% headspace prevents hydrostatic overpressure on a hot day. That is why a '500-gallon' propane tank holds about 400 gallons of propane when full, and why the common tank sizes chart below shows a working fill of 80% for every ASME propane vessel.

Does one tank calculator handle cylindrical and rectangular tanks alike?▼

Yes — pick the shape and the engine applies the exact geometry for it. For cylindrical tanks the calculator uses πr²h upright and integrates the circular segment when the cylinder lies on its side; for rectangular tanks the volume is simply length × width × height. Each result reports US and imperial gallons alongside liters and cubic meters, so one tool covers fuel, water and chemical storage without switching settings.

How do I get a rectangular tank volume in litres?▼

Measure in centimetres and litres fall out directly: length × width × height ÷ 1,000. A 120 × 50 × 60 cm rectangular tank holds 360,000 cm³ = 360 L. Working in inches, divide cubic inches by 61.024 — the 48 × 24 × 12 inch box from the formulas section is 13,104 in³ ≈ 215 L. The calculator shows litres next to every gallon figure automatically, so there is no separate conversion step.

What is the round water tank capacity calculation formula?▼

For the usual upright round water tank it is V = π × r² × h — the inside radius squared, times π, times the water height. A tank 1.8 m in diameter and 2 m tall holds π × 0.9² × 2 = 5.09 m³ ≈ 5,090 litres (about 1,345 US gallons). Measure the inside radius, keep the units consistent, and stop at the overflow shoulder if the dome does not store water. A round tank lying on its side is different: depth-to-volume becomes non-linear, so switch to the horizontal cylinder mode and the dip chart instead of the one-line formula.

How do I use the results to pick the right tank size?▼

Work backwards from demand. Add up daily use — a family of four typically needs 600–800 litres of water per day, heating oil arrives in fixed delivery increments, and propane is legally capped at 80% fill — multiply by the days of autonomy you want, and you have the target capacity. Enter candidate dimensions in the calculator until the output matches that figure, then check the standard tank sizes chart to find the nearest nameplate model. That turns the tool from a volume converter into a genuine tank size calculator for shopping comparisons.

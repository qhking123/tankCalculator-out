BTU Sizing: How to Match Your Tank to Your Appliances

The starting point for any propane tank size calculator is your appliance BTU demand. British Thermal Units per hour, or BTU/hr, is the universal measurement of how much heat each propane appliance consumes. Your gas furnace, water heater, kitchen range, clothes dryer, fireplace, and any outdoor patio heaters all have a BTU/hr rating printed on a nameplate or sticker. To find your peak total demand, add up every appliance you might run simultaneously — a winter morning with the furnace cycling on while you run the hot water, cook breakfast, and dry clothes can easily hit 170,000 to 200,000 BTU per hour in a typical American home.

Once you have your total BTU/hr, two rules of thumb guide the propane tank size selection. The first is the 275 square feet per gallon rule: each gallon of working propane capacity heats approximately 275 square feet of moderately insulated home in a climate with typical winter temperatures. A 400-gallon working capacity tank (a 500-gallon tank at 80%) therefore supports about 110,000 square feet — well over what any single-family home needs, which is why this number is a safety margin rather than a hard target.

The second rule is the runtime rule: a well-sized tank should deliver at least 7–10 days of peak demand between propane deliveries. A 500-gallon tank at 80% holds 400 gallons, and each gallon of liquid propane contains roughly 91,500 BTU. That is 400 × 91,500 = 36.6 million BTU total. At a peak demand of 170,000 BTU per hour, the tank provides about 215 hours of runtime — or nearly 9 full days of continuous max load. This matches exactly why 500-gallon tanks are the default whole-house size. Our calculator on this page runs both checks automatically when you enter your BTU demand.

The 80% Fill Rule: Legal Requirement, Not a Suggestion

If you take away one thing from this page, make it this: every propane tank, portable or stationary, must stop at 80% of its water capacity when being filled. This rule comes from the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA 58), is enforced by the Department of Transportation for all portable cylinders, and is written into every state's fire code. It is not a voluntary guideline — it is a safety mandate that every certified propane delivery driver is trained to follow.

The reason is thermal expansion, which we cover in its own section below, but the practical effect is what matters for tank sizing. A "500 gallon" propane tank does not hold 500 gallons of liquid propane. It holds 500 gallons of water — that is its geometric internal volume. At the legal 80% fill, it holds 400 gallons of liquid propane. The remaining 100 gallons of headspace allows vapor pressure to build and absorbs expansion as the liquid warms. This is why you should always size your tank based on working capacity(water capacity × 0.80), not the nominal water capacity printed on the side.

Propane Weight: 0.51× the Weight of Water

Propane is significantly lighter than water. Water weighs 8.34 pounds per US gallon at 60°F, and liquid propane weighs approximately 4.25 pounds per gallon — which is about 51% of water's weight. The industry shorthand formula is therefore:

propane weight (lb) = water gallons × 4.25

or equivalently: water gallons × 8.34 × 0.51

This weight matters for three reasons. First, it tells you how much your full tank adds to your patio, RV frame, or foundation — a full 1000-gallon stationary tank weighs over 4,000 pounds including the empty steel shell, so you need a proper concrete pad. Second, delivery companies price propane by the pound, and the weight scale on the delivery truck is how they know exactly how much they pumped into your tank. Third, you can use weight to estimate fill level on portable tanks: a 20 lb cylinder that weighs 32 pounds on your bathroom scale has about 16 pounds of propane (32 − 16 lb empty tare weight), or roughly 3.7 gallons — close to a full 80% fill.

Thermal Expansion: Why Propane Needs Headspace

Every liquid expands when warmed. Water expands about 0.02% per 10°F — almost unnoticeable. Propane is not water. Liquid propane expands approximately 1.5% in volume for every 10°F temperature rise. That is 75 times more expansion than water. This single physical property is the reason the 80% fill rule exists, and why every propane tank has a pressure relief valve (PRD) designed to vent vapor before rupture occurs.

Let's run the math on a 500-gallon tank. On a 40°F spring morning, a delivery driver fills the tank to 80% — 400 gallons of liquid propane. By mid-afternoon, the tank's temperature climbs to 90°F under direct sun. That is a 50°F rise, or five × 10°F increments. Each increment expands the liquid by 1.5%, so total expansion is 5 × 1.5% = 7.5%. Our 400 gallons of liquid has grown to 430 gallons — exactly 30 gallons short of the 500-gallon tank's total volume. The 100 gallons of ullage (headspace) we left at fill time has shrunk to 70 gallons, but the liquid still fits. If we had filled the tank to 100% that morning, the expanded liquid would be at 537.5 gallons — more than the tank can physically hold. The pressure relief valve would vent violently, spraying propane vapor that could ignite.

Thermal expansion worked example — 500 gal tank

Morning fill at 40°F: 400 gal (80% legal) — leaves 100 gal headspace

Afternoon temp rise: +50°F → 5 × 1.5% = 7.5% volume increase

Expanded liquid volume: 400 × 1.075 = 430 gal

Remaining headspace: 500 − 430 = 70 gal — still safe

If filled to 100%: 500 × 1.075 = 537.5 gal — overflow! PRD vents

Underground tanks see a milder version of this story. Buried below the frost line, tank temperatures swing less dramatically — typically 10–20°F between seasons rather than 50–60°F above ground. But even a 20°F swing causes 3% expansion on a 500-gallon tank, or 15 gallons of extra liquid. The headspace handles that easily. Never be tempted to fill past 80% in winter because "the cold keeps it down." Spring is coming, and the ground will warm.

How to Check How Much Propane Is In Your Tank

Stationary tanks have a gauge — either a direct-read needle gauge or a wireless magnetic sensor that transmits to a mobile app. Portable cylinders require a different approach. The simplest and most accurate method is the warm water test: pour hot water down the side of the tank, then run your hand vertically up the metal. The tank wall is warm above the liquid level and cool where liquid propane is present — the propane is cold because it is evaporating inside the cylinder. Mark the transition point with a felt-tip pen, then measure the height and compare to the total height. A tank that is half covered with cool metal is roughly 50% full.

The scale method is even more precise if you know the tare weight (empty weight) of your cylinder, which is stamped into the tank collar. A standard 20 lb cylinder has a tare weight of 16 pounds. Weigh it on a bathroom scale, subtract 16, and divide by 4.25 — that gives you gallons remaining. A 20 lb cylinder weighing 29 pounds has 13 pounds of propane, or about 3.06 gallons remaining — roughly 77% of its 4-gallon working capacity.

Common Mistakes in Propane Tank Size Calculation

1. Sizing on Water Capacity Instead of Working Capacity

The single most common mistake. A homeowner shopping for a "500-gallon propane tank" thinks they are getting 500 gallons of usable propane. They are getting 400 gallons. If you calculated your BTU runtime on 500 gallons, you are overestimating by 20%. Always multiply water capacity by 0.80 first, then do your BTU and heating-area math. Every number in our calculator on this page uses working capacity — you can trust it.

2. Ignoring Peak vs. Average Demand

Your furnace cycles on and off all day — it only runs about 30–50% of the time in mild weather. But the tank needs to handle peak demand, not average. On a zero-degree morning when the furnace is running continuously while you also heat water, cook breakfast, and run a dryer, your peak BTU/hr is 100% of every appliance's rating. If you size the tank to average load, you will run out of propane on the coldest day of the year. Always sum the BTU/hr of all simultaneously-running appliances for sizing.

3. Forgetting That Propane Weighs Less Than Water

When you look at a delivery receipt, you will see "gallons delivered" and you might assume each gallon weighs 8.34 pounds. It does not — it weighs 4.25 pounds. A 100-gallon delivery adds about 425 pounds to your tank, not 834. This matters if you are monitoring fill by weight on a stationary tank pad scale, or if you are trying to estimate remaining gallons from a portable cylinder's total weight. Divide propane weight by 4.25 to get gallons, not by 8.34.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does a 500 gallon propane tank only hold 400 gallons of propane?▼

Propane tanks are legally limited to an 80% maximum fill by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) and federal DOT regulations. The remaining 20% is called the ullage space and exists because liquid propane expands significantly when warmed — about 1.5% per 10°F temperature rise. A full 500-gallon tank at 60°F would expand by roughly 5 gallons if the temperature climbed to 100°F, which would leave no room for vapor pressure and could rupture the relief valve. The 80% rule is not a suggestion — every propane delivery driver is trained to stop filling at that mark, and stationary tanks are built with a fixed liquid level gauge that physically prevents over-fill.

What exactly is a 20 lb propane tank?▼

A 20 lb tank is the most common portable cylinder for gas grills. The "20 lb" refers to the weight of liquid propane it holds when filled to 80% capacity, not the tank's empty or full weight. A 20 lb cylinder has a water capacity of 5 gallons. At 80% fill that is 4 gallons of liquid propane. Propane weighs about 4.25 pounds per gallon (0.51 × water weight of 8.34 lb/gal), so 4 gallons × 4.25 lb/gal = 17 lbs — plus a small rounding factor in the industry standard. The empty tank itself weighs about 16 pounds, so a full 20 lb cylinder on your scale should read roughly 36–37 pounds total. If your tank weighs more than that, it was overfilled, which is unsafe.

How do I size a propane tank from my appliance BTU demand?▼

Add up the BTU per hour rating of every propane appliance you might run simultaneously — your furnace, water heater, range, dryer, and any fireplaces or patio heaters — then use two sizing rules. First, the 275 ft² per gallon rule: each gallon of working capacity can heat roughly 275 square feet of moderately insulated space in a typical climate. Second, the runtime rule: a well-sized tank should provide at least 7–10 days of full-load runtime between deliveries. A typical American home with a 100,000 BTU furnace, 40,000 BTU water heater, and occasional use of a 30,000 BTU fireplace peaks at about 170,000 BTU per hour. A 500-gallon tank with 400 gallons working capacity at 91,500 BTU/gal delivers about 36.6 million BTU — enough for roughly 215 continuous hours of peak demand, or nearly 9 days. That is why 500 gallons is the most popular whole-house size.

How much does propane weigh compared to water?▼

Liquid propane is about 51% the weight of water. Water weighs 8.34 pounds per US gallon at 60°F, so propane weighs approximately 4.25 pounds per gallon (the industry often rounds this to 4.2 lb/gal for quick math). The exact density of propane varies slightly with temperature — it gets lighter as it warms — but 4.25 lb/gal is the standard number used for tank sizing and delivery calculations. This is why a 20 lb tank holds roughly 4 gallons of liquid propane: 4 × 4.25 = 17 lbs, rounded up to the 20 lb marketing label to account for temperature variations.

Why is there an 80% fill rule for propane tanks?▼

The 80% fill rule exists to protect against thermal expansion. Liquid propane expands about 1.5% in volume for every 10°F temperature increase. A tank filled to 100% on a cool 40°F morning would need to expand by roughly 9% if the temperature climbed to 100°F in the afternoon. Without ullage space, that expanding liquid would have nowhere to go except to push open the pressure relief valve — or, in the worst case, rupture the tank. The 20% headspace gives the vapor room to compress and absorb the expanding liquid. This is not a fire code triviality; every year, fire marshals investigate tank ruptures caused by over-filling, especially with underground residential tanks where the ground temperature can swing 40–60°F between seasons.

How many BTUs are in a gallon of propane?▼

One US gallon of liquid propane contains approximately 91,500 British Thermal Units (BTUs) of energy content. This number is standardized by the American Gas Association and assumes propane at a standard reference temperature of 60°F. The exact value can vary from about 90,500 to 92,500 BTU/gal depending on the precise grade of propane, but 91,500 is the number used by every appliance manufacturer, HVAC engineer, and propane company in North America. To convert a tank's total liquid gallons into BTUs, simply multiply by 91,500.

Can I fill a propane tank more than 80% in winter?▼

No — the 80% fill rule applies year-round for stationary and portable cylinders. Even in cold weather, liquid propane will warm up once the tank is brought indoors or exposed to direct sunlight. A tank filled to 90% on a 20°F day would expand by more than 12 gallons when brought up to 100°F, which is enough to trip the PRD (pressure relief device) violently, or worse. There is a myth that winter allows higher fills because "the cold keeps it contracted" — but the tank cannot stay cold forever. Every professional propane driver is certified to stop at 80% regardless of season.

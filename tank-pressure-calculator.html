Every question about a tank holding water — how hard it pushes on the glass, what a spigot at the bottom delivers, whether a stand or frame is doing real work — starts with one physical quantity: hydrostatic pressure. It is one of the friendliest formulas in all of physics, needing nothing but a tape measure and one constant. This guide walks through the pressure math in plain language, converts liquid level to psi, and then goes one step further to the number people actually care about when a tank wall creaks: the total force in pounds.

If you came here from a sizing question — “how many gallons is this tank, anyway?” — start with the tank volume calculator to nail the geometry first, then come back for the pressure side. Pressure and volume answer different questions: volume tells you what the tank holds, pressure tells you what the holding costs.

How to Calculate Pressure on the Side of a Tank

The rule is P = ρ·g·h: pressure equals density times gravity times depth. Read it out loud and it says something wonderfully simple — the only thing that matters is how deep you are. A square inch of glass 10 inches below the water line feels the weight of the 10-inch column of water standing on top of it, and nothing else. Widen the tank, empty half of it, decorate it with rocks — the pressure at 10 inches never changes, because the column of water above that square inch is the same 10 inches of water either way.

For water in US units, the formula collapses into a single memorable conversion. A column of freshwater exactly 27.68 inches tall weighs 1 pound per square inch. So the pressure at any depth is:

psi = (depth in inches ÷ 27.68) × SG

P = ρ·g·h, where SG is the specific gravity — 1.000 for freshwater, 1.025 for saltwater

The 27.68-Inch Water Column Rule

Where does 27.68 come from? One cubic foot of freshwater weighs 62.4 pounds and spreads over 144 square inches of floor, so each inch-tall slice of water presses down with 62.4 ÷ 144 = 0.4335 psi. One pound of pressure therefore needs 1 ÷ 0.4335 = 2.307 feet of water — which is 27.68 inches. HVAC technicians still quote duct and gas pressures in “inches of water column” for exactly this reason. Saltwater weighs 64.0 pounds per cubic foot, so its slice is heavier: 0.4443 psi per foot, and only 27.05 inches of it needed per psi. For mental math, keep the friendlier rounding: every 2.31 feet of water adds about 1 psi.

💡 Tip: To estimate psi without a calculator, divide the depth in feet by 2.31. A 30-inch-deep barrel is 2.5 ft → about 1.08 psi at the bottom. A 6-ft stock tank → about 2.6 psi. Saltwater: divide by 2.25 instead.

How Level Transmitters Use This Relationship

Industrial plants exploit the table in reverse. A submersible pressure transducer bolted near the bottom of a chemical tank does not measure level at all — it measures psi, then the controller multiplies by 27.68 ÷ SG to report a level in inches. The same trick runs rain-harvesting cisterns, bulk water delivery trucks, and the brine tanks in water softeners. It even explains why a “0–1 psi” gauge on a 27-inch-deep sump is a precision instrument while the same gauge on a fire hydrant would be laughably coarse: the full scale of the sensor should match the depth range you expect, since each psi buys you 27.68 inches of reading.

Fish Tank Water Pressure Calculator: a Worked 55-Gallon Example

The classic 55-gallon aquarium — 48 inches long, 13 inches front-to-back, 21 inches tall — is the perfect specimen because its numbers are famous and its wall loads are widely underestimated. Fill it to the brim with freshwater and run the wall-force mode above with L = 48 and h = 21. Here is what the physics says, step by step:

Worked example: 48″ × 13″ × 21″ freshwater tank

Convert: L = 48 ÷ 12 = 4.0 ft, h = 21 ÷ 12 = 1.75 ft

Bottom pressure: 21 ÷ 27.68 = 0.759 psi

Average wall pressure: 0.4335 × (1.75 ÷ 2) = 0.379 psi

Long-wall force: ½ × 62.4 × 4.0 × 1.75² = 382 lb

Center of pressure: 21 ÷ 3 = 7 in above the bottom

End panel (13″ wide): ½ × 62.4 × (13 ÷ 12) × 1.75² = 103.5 lb

All four walls: 2 × 382 + 2 × 103.5 ≈ 971 lb

Bottom pane: 0.759 psi × 48 × 13 = 474 lb straight down

Note what the long wall carries: 382 pounds pushing outward on a single pane, even though the peak pressure is a modest 0.76 psi. That is the volume effect — 4 feet × 1.75 feet is 7 square feet, or 1,008 square inches of glass, and even a gentle average of 0.379 psi times 1,008 squares up fast. If your tank dimensions came from a sticker rather than a tape, confirm them with the fish tank volume calculator first — geometry errors propagate straight into the force math.

Why Aquarium Panels Bow Outward

Glass is stiff but not rigid, and the load on it is not uniform. Pressure is zero at the water surface and peaks at the bottom, so a panel flexes most just below its vertical midpoint, near the center of pressure. The result is the familiar outward bulge on long, thin panes. Manufacturers answer with two levers: thicker glass, whose stiffness grows with the cube of its thickness, and center braces that tie the two long walls together so they push against each other rather than flexing freely. This is also why tank height, more than length, drives glass cost — every added inch of depth loads the bottom edge disproportionately, because force scales with the depth squared, not the depth itself.

Calculate Force on an Aquarium Tank Wall: Why Depth Is Squared

Pressure is a point property; force is what a whole panel accumulates. Sum the triangular pressure distribution across a wall of length L and depth h and you get the closed form:

F = ½ · ρ·g · L · h²

in US units: F (lb) = 0.5 × 62.4 × SG × L(ft) × h(ft)² — the ½ folds the triangular profile in

The h² is the part intuition misses. Doubling a wall’s length doubles the force — linear, as expected. But doubling the water depth quadruples the force: more pressure at every point and more area to push on, compounding. One extra inch of depth on a 48-inch wall sounds trivial and is not: going from 20 to 21 inches of water raises the long-wall force from 347 lb to 382 lb — an extra 35 pounds for free.

⚠️ Warning — force scales with h²: the same 48-inch wall at 30 inches of water instead of 21 carries 0.5 × 62.4 × 4 × (2.5²) = 780 lb versus 382 lb — roughly 2× the force for only a 1.43× increase in depth. “Just a few inches deeper” is how stock tanks, pond windows, and DIY acrylic builds get in trouble.

The practical takeaway for tank placement: the stand and the floor care about total weight (water, glass, substrate), but the rim, braces, and silicone seams care about wall force. A 125-gallon tank filled to 20 inches on a 72-inch wall carries about 520 lb per long pane — which is why commercially built tanks that tall arrive with 10–12 mm glass and double bracing as standard equipment.

How to Calculate Fluid Pressure in a Tank: The Hydrostatic Paradox

Here is the result that surprises nearly everyone the first time: pressure inside a tank depends only on depth — never on volume, never on shape. A 5-gallon bucket filled to 20 inches and a 200-gallon show tank filled to 20 inches both read exactly 20 ÷ 27.68 = 0.72 psi at the bottom. The bucket holds 40 pounds of water; the tank holds 800 — yet the pressure at the base is identical, because pressure is weight per area, and the tall skinny column standing over one square inch of bucket-bottom weighs the same as the squat fat column over one square inch of tank-bottom. This is the hydrostatic paradox, and “paradox” is a joke: there is nothing contradictory about it, the arithmetic is just stubborn.

The rule reaches further than flat-bottomed rectangles. A cone-bottom hopper, a tall cylinder, and a weird custom hexagon all obey it identically: probe any point and the local pressure is ρ·g·h, where h is the vertical distance down from the free surface. It is also why the calculator above asks for a depth and a liquid and resolutely refuses to ask for gallons — the gallons are irrelevant to pressure. What volume does control is how long that pressure keeps flowing once you open a valve, which is the whole subject of the tank drain time calculator — drain flow starts from this pressure, then spends it as the level falls.

When Shape Does Matter: Pressure Versus Total Force

The paradox has one honest fine print. Pressure per square inch ignores shape — but the total force on a container’s walls is pressure integrated over area, and area is all about shape. Two tanks at the same 20-inch depth have the same bottom pressure, yet if one has triple the footprint, its bottom pane carries triple the total force, and its longer walls carry more as well. That is why a 300-gallon pond at 0.72 psi can burst a weak seam that a bucket at 0.72 psi never could: same pressure, wildly different number of square inches to fail at once. Pressure diagnoses the material; force diagnoses the structure.

How to Calculate Pressure on the Sides of a Tank: All Four Walls

A rectangular tank has four wetted walls and no two of them necessarily match. The two long walls (length L) take the big force, ½·ρ·g·L·h². The two end panels (width W) take the same formula with W swapped for L — for the 55-gallon example that is 103.5 lb per end versus 382 lb per side, because force scales with length at fixed depth. Add them up and the four walls together carry about 971 lb of outward push, held in pure tension by the silicone seams and resisted by the top rim acting like a stretchy belt.

Two asymmetries are worth internalizing. First, the bottom pane is the only wall seeing uniform pressure — 0.759 psi everywhere on the 55-gallon’s base, a steady 474 lb straight down through the stand. Second, cylindrical tanks convert the same pressure into pure hoop tension instead of bending, which is why round stock tanks and rain barrels get away with dramatically thinner walls per pound of water: bending is expensive, tension is cheap. Whenever a design lets you trade a flat wall for a curved one at the same depth, the curved one wins the material budget.

Tank Pressure FAQ

How much psi is at the bottom of a 4-ft tank?▼

A 4-foot water column is 48 inches, and 27.68 inches of freshwater equals 1 psi. So the bottom of a 4-ft freshwater tank sees 48 ÷ 27.68 = 1.73 psi gauge. Add atmospheric pressure and the absolute pressure is about 16.43 psi. Fill it with saltwater instead and the gauge reading rises roughly 2.5% to 1.78 psi, because saltwater has a specific gravity of 1.025 versus 1.000 for freshwater. Those are small numbers — pressure is rarely the problem; the total force spread across a long wall is what matters.

What is the formula for pressure on a tank side wall?▼

Pressure at a single point is P = ρ·g·h: density times gravity times depth. In US units for water, that simplifies to psi = (depth in inches ÷ 27.68) × specific gravity. A wall does not feel one pressure, though — it feels pressure growing from zero at the surface to a maximum at the bottom. So engineers use the average pressure, which equals the bottom pressure divided by 2: 0.4335 × SG × (depth in feet ÷ 2). Multiply that average by the wall area to get the total force, or use the direct formula F = ½·ρ·g·L·h².

Does the total volume of water change the pressure?▼

No — this is the hydrostatic paradox. Pressure depends only on depth, not on how much water sits above the point. A 5-gallon bucket filled to 20 inches and a 200-gallon tank filled to 20 inches both read 20 ÷ 27.68 = 0.72 psi at the bottom. The bucket holds far less water, yet at the bottom face the pressure is identical. Volume matters for total force, because force multiplies pressure by area — a big tank has more wall area, so the same pressure loads far more square inches of glass.

Saltwater vs freshwater — how big is the psi difference?▼

About 2.5%, because saltwater's specific gravity is 1.025 versus 1.000 for freshwater. Per foot of depth that is 0.4443 psi versus 0.4335 psi. At a 24-inch depth, freshwater gives 0.867 psi gauge and saltwater gives 0.889 psi — a difference of 0.022 psi you could not feel with your hand. The difference compounds only through the depth term: at 10 feet, freshwater reads 4.34 psi and saltwater 4.44 psi. For wall force, saltwater adds roughly 3% — the 382 lb long-wall load of a 55-gallon becomes about 395 lb in a marine setup.

How do I convert liquid level to psi?▼

For freshwater, divide the level in inches by 27.68. A 12-inch level gives 0.43 psi, 36 inches gives 1.30 psi, and 48 inches gives 1.73 psi. For any other liquid, divide by 27.68 and multiply by the specific gravity — a 40-inch column of a syrup with SG 1.3 gives 40 ÷ 27.68 × 1.3 = 1.88 psi. These are gauge readings, relative to the atmosphere. If a sensor or specification wants absolute pressure, add 14.7 psi. Every 2.31 feet of water adds exactly 1 psi, which is the fastest mental shortcut.

Why do aquarium tank walls bow outward?▼

Water pressure rises linearly with depth, so every glass panel is pushed hardest at its bottom edge and not at all at the surface. That uneven load makes the panel flex outward most near the middle-bottom. A standard 55-gallon tank's 48-inch long wall holding 21 inches of water carries about 382 pounds of total force, concentrated at the center of pressure 7 inches above the bottom. Deeper tanks bow much faster because force scales with depth squared — a 30-inch wall of the same length carries roughly 780 pounds, double the 21-inch load for only a 1.43× depth increase. Thicker glass and center braces counteract this.

Gauge vs absolute pressure — which one applies to my tank?▼

Gauge pressure is measured relative to the atmosphere and is what a tank wall actually responds to. An open, vented tank has air pushing on the water surface and on the outside of the glass at 14.7 psi, which cancels out — only the water's own weight, the gauge part, bends the panel. Absolute pressure adds the 14.7 psi of atmosphere on top: a 24-inch freshwater depth is 0.867 psi gauge but 15.57 psi absolute. Use gauge numbers for wall loads, pump head, and spigot flow; use absolute when a sealed sensor datasheet requires it.

How much pressure does a rain barrel spigot get?▼

A full 55-gallon rain barrel stands about 3 to 4 feet tall, so the bottom sees 1.30 to 1.73 psi gauge. A spigot mounted 6 inches above the base sees the pressure of roughly 30 to 42 inches of water above it — about 1.1 to 1.5 psi. That is only around 2–3% of typical city water pressure (40–60 psi), which is why gravity-fed soaker hoses run slowly and drip-irrigation works while spray nozzles do not. Elevating the barrel on a stand helps linearly: every added foot of height buys another 0.43 psi.

What does center of pressure mean?▼

Because pressure on a vertical wall grows from zero at the surface to a maximum at the bottom, the total force does not act at the wall's midpoint. It acts lower — at the center of pressure, located one third of the depth up from the bottom. For a 21-inch water depth, that is 7 inches above the base. This is why tank rims, bottom trim, and lower braces take disproportionate load, and why bows in over-stressed glass appear just below the vertical middle. For design-style rules of thumb, remember: average pressure sits at half depth, but the resultant force sits at one third depth.

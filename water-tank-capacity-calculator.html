Why Water Tank Capacity Matters — From Well Pressure Tanks to Rainwater Cisterns

Water tanks come in dozens of shapes and serve every need — a 200-liter polyethylene tank under a kitchen sink, a 1,000-liter well pressure tank in a rural basement, a 2,500-liter rainwater tank beside a suburban house, or a 50,000-liter fire suppression cistern at a commercial warehouse. Getting the volume right is not just an academic exercise. An undersized well tank means your pump cycles on and off every minute, wearing out the pressure switch in 18 months instead of 10 years. An oversized rainwater tank wastes yard space and costs twice as much as it needs to. And guessing the full weight of a tank before pouring a slab is how you end up with a cracked patio six months after install. This water tank capacity calculator gives you the exact liters, gallons, full weight and surface area for round, square and rectangular tanks in one click — so you can spec the right tank, the right slab, and the right pump the first time.

The rest of this page walks you through the formulas with worked examples you can follow on a scrap of paper, explains how to convert between liters and gallons without looking it up every time, gives you a standard sizes chart for common household and commercial tanks, shows a water weight table so you can estimate slab loading in your head, and covers the big installation decisions: above-ground versus underground, well tank sizing rules, and how to read a manufacturer's spec sheet without getting caught by nominal versus actual dimensions.

Round Water Tank Formula: V = π r² h — Explained with Worked Examples

The round (or cylindrical) water tank is by far the most common shape for household storage. Every well pressure tank, every standard rainwater tank, and most elevated water towers are vertical cylinders. The formula for the volume of a cylinder is one of the most basic in geometry — the area of the circular base multiplied by the height. Written out:

V = π × r² × h

Where V is volume, r is the inside radius of the tank (half the inside diameter), and h is the inside height. Use inside dimensions — not the outside numbers printed on the shipping box. A typical steel tank has walls 4–6 mm thick, which subtracts 8–12 mm from the inside diameter and another 4–6 mm from the inside height. Polyethylene tanks are thinner at 2–3 mm but still not zero. If you only have outside dimensions, subtract twice the wall thickness from the diameter and once from the height before applying the formula.

The easiest units to work in are meters. Volume in cubic meters converts directly to liters because 1 m³ = 1,000 L. If you are working in feet, 1 cubic foot equals 7.48 US gallons, and 1 cubic inch equals 1 / 231 US gallons. The calculator above handles all the unit conversion for you — you can enter meters or inches and get both liters and gallons back.

Worked Example 1 — 1.8 m × 2.0 m Round Tank

Scenario: a standard 1000 L well pressure tank

Inside diameter: 1.8 m

Inside height: 2.0 m

Inside radius r: 1.8 ÷ 2 = 0.9 m

V = π × 0.9² × 2.0

= π × 0.81 × 2.0

= π × 1.62

= 5.09 m³

× 1000 = 5,090 liters

× 0.26417 = 1,344 US gallons

A tank this size holds roughly 1,100 kg of water when full. Most tanks of this nominal 1000 L size have a usable pressure range of 20–60 psi and about a third of the volume is trapped air cushion, so the drawdown is closer to 330–380 L.

Worked Example 2 — 3.0 m × 4.0 m Round Tank

Scenario: a large rainwater cistern for a family home

Inside diameter: 3.0 m

Inside height: 4.0 m

Inside radius r: 3.0 ÷ 2 = 1.5 m

V = π × 1.5² × 4.0

= π × 2.25 × 4.0

= π × 9.0

= 28.27 m³

× 1000 = 28,270 liters

× 0.26417 = 7,468 US gallons

Worked Example 3 — 2.4 m × 3.0 m Round Tank

Scenario: an above-ground fire suppression tank

Inside diameter: 2.4 m

Inside height: 3.0 m

Inside radius r: 2.4 ÷ 2 = 1.2 m

V = π × 1.2² × 3.0

= π × 1.44 × 3.0

= π × 4.32

Rectangular Cistern: V = l × w × h

Rectangular water tanks are most commonly built on-site — concrete block cisterns, underground tanks excavated into a basement corner, or steel-panel tanks bolted together in a shipping yard. The formula is even simpler than the cylinder: multiply the three inside dimensions. No π, no square roots, no geometry homework.

V = length × width × height

Because rectangular tanks are almost always custom-built, the wall thickness matters more than it does for a factory-made PE tank. A typical concrete block cistern uses 150 mm (6 inch) hollow blocks filled with rebar and grout. That means the inside dimensions are 300 mm shorter in each horizontal direction than the outside footprint, and 150 mm shorter in height. Measure the inside of the blocks before pouring the slab — not after the walls go up.

Worked example: 2.4 m × 1.2 m × 1.8 m rectangular cistern

Inside length: 2.4 m

Inside width: 1.2 m

Inside height: 1.8 m

V = 2.4 × 1.2 × 1.8 = 5.184 m³

= 5,184 liters

= 1,370 US gallons

Full water weight: 5,184 kg ≈ 5.2 tonnes — which is about the same as a small car.

Square Tank — Same Math as Rectangular

A square water tank is just a rectangular tank where the length equals the width. If the side is s and the height is h, the formula collapses to V = s² × h. Square tanks are less common than round or rectangular ones because a round tank gives more volume per surface area of wall material — but square tanks fit neatly in corners, stack more easily in a warehouse, and are sometimes built above ground with wooden or composite panels for rainwater harvesting on a budget.

Worked example: 1.0 m × 1.0 m × 1.5 m square tank

Inside side: 1.0 m

Inside height: 1.5 m

V = 1.0² × 1.5 = 1.5 m³

= 1,500 liters

= 396 US gallons

Surface Area — Why It Matters for Slab and Base Design

The calculator on this page also returns the total surface area of the tank. You might be tempted to ignore that number — after all, you came for liters and gallons — but surface area is the key input for two critical design decisions: how much concrete you need for a supporting slab, and how much paint or liner you need if you are refurbishing an old steel tank.

For a round tank, surface area is the sum of the circular top, the circular bottom, and the cylindrical side wall: A = 2πr² + 2πrh. For a square tank it is the two square ends plus the four rectangular sides: A = 2s² + 4sh. For a rectangular tank it is all six faces: A = 2(lw + lh + wh). The calculator on this page gives surface area in square centimeters and square meters — use the square meter number for paint coverage (most tank epoxies cover 4–6 m² per liter) and for slab footprint sizing.

Practical example: slab sizing for a 1000 L round tank

Diameter: 0.98 m, height: 1.3 m

Footprint area = π × (0.49)² = 0.754 m²

Full tank water weight = 1,000 kg ≈ 2,205 lb

Tank shell (PE) ≈ 50 kg total load ≈ 1,050 kg

Load per m² = 1,050 ÷ 0.754 = 1,393 kg/m²

A standard 100 mm residential slab is rated for 2,000 kg/m² — comfortably above this. For steel tanks 5,000 L and up, consider thickening to 150 mm or adding rebar.

Water Weight When Full — 1 L = 1 kg, 1 US gal ≈ 8.34 lb

The most useful mental shortcut in water tank engineering: one liter of water weighs exactly one kilogram at standard temperature and pressure. That is not an approximation — it is how the kilogram was originally defined in the late 1700s (the mass of one liter of water at its maximum density). US customary units give you 8.33 pounds per gallon, rounded up to 8.34 for quick math. If you remember those two numbers, you can estimate the full weight of any tank in your head: multiply liters by 1 to get kilograms, multiply gallons by 8.34 to get pounds.

Water weight is always the dominant component of total system weight. The 5000 L tank you are considering puts 5 tonnes (5,000 kg) of water on the ground — plus another 100–200 kg for the polyethylene shell, or 800–1,200 kg for steel. Before you order a tank, grab your calculator and run: (liters × 1.0) + (shell weight estimate) × 1.5 safety factor = minimum slab load rating.

Above-Ground vs Underground Water Tanks — What the Formula Does Not Tell You

The geometry of a water tank is the same whether you bury it or set it on a slab — the volume formula does not care about elevation. But the installation math absolutely does. An above-ground tank needs a solid slab, UV protection if it is polyethylene, and insulation if winters get below zero. An underground tank needs excavation, a pump to move water back up to the house, a concrete base to prevent floating during heavy rain, and a leak detection system because a buried tank springing a leak is invisible until the lawn above turns into a swamp.

🏠

Above-Ground Tank Pros

  • Lower upfront cost — no excavation
  • Easy to inspect and clean the inside
  • Gravity feeds water to house (no pump needed for short runs)
  • Available off-the-shelf in every size
  • No permit required in most rural areas

Cons

  • Freezes in sub-zero winters unless insulated
  • Takes up yard space
  • Translucent PE tanks grow algae in full sun
  • Visible — some homeowners find it ugly
🌱

Underground Tank Pros

  • Water stays cool year-round (10–15 °C stable)
  • No above-ground yard footprint
  • No freezing issues in climates above −10 °C
  • Protected from UV damage and vandalism
  • Can be installed much larger without permitting

Cons

  • 2–3× higher installed cost
  • Requires a pump to lift water to the house
  • Leak is invisible until ground saturates
  • Risk of floating during flood events
  • Hard to clean or repair

Frequently Asked Questions — Water Tank Capacity

Above-ground vs underground water tank: which is better?▼

Above-ground tanks cost less to install (no excavation), are easier to inspect and clean, and let gravity feed your home without an extra pump. On the downside, they freeze in cold climates unless insulated, take up yard space, and algae can grow in translucent tanks if left in full sun. Underground tanks keep water cool year-round, save above-ground space, and avoid freezing issues in mild zones — but they cost 2–3 times more to install, require a pump to get water back up to the house, and any leak is impossible to see until the ground above turns muddy. Most rural homes choose underground for large 2500–5000 L cisterns; urban properties with limited yard space stick to above-ground polyethylene tanks up to 1000 L.

How do I size a well water tank for my family?▼

A standard pressure tank for a private well should hold 10–15 gallons per person in the household for a day of storage, plus a 50% safety margin. A family of four needs roughly 4 × 12 × 1.5 = 72 gallons, which rounds up to a standard 80-gallon (300 L) pressure tank. Larger storage cisterns that supplement well water use a different rule: plan 50–80 gallons per person per day for a 3–7 day reserve, so a family of four with a 5-day backup wants 4 × 65 × 5 = 1,300 gallons (about 5,000 L). The calculator on this page works out any tank size — just pick your shape, enter dimensions, and convert the result back to gallons to cross-check against these rules.

How much does a full water tank weigh, and what slab do I need?▼

One liter of water weighs exactly one kilogram; one US gallon weighs about 8.34 pounds. So a 1000 L round tank weighs 1000 kg (2205 lb) when full, plus the tank shell itself — a polyethylene tank adds roughly 40–60 kg, a steel tank adds 80–120 kg, and a concrete cistern shell can weigh over a tonne empty. For above-ground installations the supporting slab must be able to carry the full operating weight plus a safety factor of 1.5. A 5000 L tank sitting on a 3 m × 3 m slab puts about 5500 kg on 9 square meters, or 611 kg/m² — well within a standard 100 mm thick residential concrete slab rated for 2000 kg/m². Always place a plastic damp-proof membrane between the tank bottom and concrete to prevent corrosion on metal tanks and condensation issues on polyethylene.

What is the formula for a round water tank volume?▼

The volume of a cylindrical (round) water tank is V = π × r² × h, where r is the inside radius and h is the inside height. Working in meters gives cubic meters; multiply by 1000 to get liters. A tank with diameter 1.8 m (radius 0.9 m) and height 2.0 m holds π × 0.9² × 2.0 = 5.09 m³ = 5090 L. A larger tank at 3 m diameter (radius 1.5 m) and 4 m height holds π × 1.5² × 4 = 28.27 m³ = 28,270 L. Always use inside dimensions, not the manufacturer's nominal outside numbers — a steel tank with 5 mm walls loses about 1% of diameter on each side.

How do you calculate rectangular water tank capacity?▼

Rectangular cisterns use the simple formula V = l × w × h for inside dimensions. If you have a tank that is 2.4 m long, 1.2 m wide and 1.8 m tall, the volume is 2.4 × 1.2 × 1.8 = 5.184 m³ = 5,184 liters. Convert that to US gallons by multiplying by 264.172 — about 1,370 gal. Rectangular tanks are usually built in-place from concrete blocks, so the wall thickness is 100–150 mm on each side; subtract twice the wall thickness from each horizontal dimension before applying the formula.

What size tank do I need for rainwater harvesting?▼

Rainwater tank size depends on two numbers: your average annual rainfall and the total roof area that feeds the tank. The formula is roof area (m²) × annual rainfall (m) × 0.9 (runoff coefficient, accounting for first-flush and evaporation) × 1000 to convert to liters. A 150 m² roof in a location with 800 mm of annual rain collects about 150 × 0.8 × 0.9 × 1000 = 108,000 L of water per year. You do not need a tank that big — a 5,000–10,000 L tank buffers you through dry spells, and any overflow goes to stormwater. The calculator on this page gives you the capacity of whatever size you are considering so you can match it against your annual catchment.
